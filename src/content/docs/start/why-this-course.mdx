## What a model actually does

Strip away the vocabulary and a neural network is a function from numbers to numbers. Text goes in as a list of
numbers (one row per token), an image as a grid of numbers (one per pixel per channel), and a list of numbers comes
out: which token is likely next, which pixel colour, which class. Between the two ends sits a chain of very simple
steps, repeated an enormous number of times.

The step is this: take two lists of numbers, multiply matching entries, add the products up.

$$
a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3
$$

That is the dot product. One multiplication followed by one addition into a running sum is called a *multiply-add*,
and it is the only arithmetic a model does in bulk. A **layer** of a network is thousands of dot products of the same
input with different lists of numbers (the layer's *weights*). **Attention** is dot products between the rows that
represent tokens, asking "how much does this word look like that one". An **embedding** is just the list of numbers a
token is turned into so that dot products can be taken at all.

How many? The 2020 scaling-laws paper from OpenAI (Kaplan et al., *Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models*,
arXiv:2001.08361, January 2020) gives the rule of thumb: a forward pass costs about $2N$ floating-point operations
per token, where $N$ is the number of parameters outside the embedding tables, and the factor 2 is exactly "one
multiply and one add". So, per parameter, per token: one multiply-add. GPT-2 small (2019) has 124 million parameters,
about 85 million of them outside the embeddings, so producing **one token** takes roughly 85 million multiply-adds,
plus the attention over the context. A 300-token answer is about 25 billion of them. Llama 3 8B (Meta, April 2024)
does about 8 billion multiply-adds per token; the figure scales with the parameter count, which is why the count is
the number everyone quotes.

Those are big numbers, but every one of them is the same small operation you can draw on a napkin. The whole course
is about that operation: what it means, how it is stacked into matrices and tensors, how a model's numbers are chosen
(by taking derivatives of a loss), and why the loss looks the way it does (probability and "why squares everywhere").

## You already do this

If you have written a lighting shader, you have computed dot products thousands of times: `max(0.0, dot(N, L))`
is the brightness of a surface, and it is the dot product of the normal with the light direction. The course's
first real lesson on the dot product derives it from the law of cosines and shows exactly why that number is a cosine.

Other things you know, which the course reuses on purpose:

| what you do in CG | what it is in this course |
|---|---|
| `dot(N, L)` in a shader | the dot product (Part 1); cosine similarity between embeddings is the same formula |
| a 4x4 transform matrix, `xform` nodes, parent spaces | a matrix as "where the basis vectors land" (Part 2); a layer of a network is a matrix applied to every token |
| normals, tangents, the cross product | orthogonality, bases, projections (Parts 1 and 2) |
| blend shapes: `base + w1 * delta1 + w2 * delta2` | a linear combination (lesson 1.2); the output of a layer is a linear combination of its weight columns |
| a Graph Editor curve and its tangent handles | a function and its derivative (Part 4); gradient descent follows the tangent downhill |
| an image as H x W x C, a point cloud as N x 3 | tensors, shapes and strides (Part 3) |
| half-float textures, 8-bit normals, the banding they cause | floating-point formats and quantization (Branch Q), which decide whether a model fits your GPU |

None of this is a metaphor. The dot product in the shader and the dot product in attention are the same function,
with longer lists.

## The spine of the course in one sentence

Measure distance (Pythagoras) → measure angle (the law of cosines) → arrows as lists of numbers (vectors) →
compare two arrows (the dot product) → move all arrows at once (matrices) → stack many arrows (tensors) → a layer
$y = xW^{\mathsf{T}} + b$ → how wrong the layer is (a loss) → how to change $W$ (derivatives, the gradient) → why this
loss (probability, statistics, "why squares") → train a machine (regression, then a small network) → attention and a
transformer block, built only from parts you already own.

Every lesson answers the question the previous lesson left open, and ends with the next question. The next page,
[How it all connects](lesson:how-it-connects), shows the same sentence as one animation.

## The parts, and what you will be able to do

**Part 0, Ground.** Squares as areas, roots, powers, distance, angles, the unit circle, the law of cosines.
*You will be able to* read $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $x^{-1/2}$ as pictures, and see where $\cos\theta$ comes from.

**Part 1, Vectors.** Arrows and lists of numbers, length, the dot product, projection, bases, cosine similarity and
embeddings, the DFT as a list of dot products. *You will be able to* explain `dot(N, L)` to a junior, read an
embedding-search result as an angle, and know why a normalized vector has length one.

**Part 2, Matrices.** Transformations, columns as images of the basis, four views of matrix multiplication,
determinants, systems of equations, inverses, eigenvectors. *You will be able to* read `y = x @ W.T + b` as "every
token times every weight column", and know what a 4x4 matrix does without multiplying it out.

**Part 3, Tensors.** Shapes, axes, batches, images and text as tensors, strides over flat memory, broadcasting,
reductions, einsum. *You will be able to* read a shape like `(B, T, D)` and an error like "cannot broadcast
(8, 3) with (4,)" and know what to fix.

**Parts 4 to 6, Change and accumulation.** Derivatives as tangent zoom, the chain rule, gradients, Jacobians,
gradient descent, the backpropagation graph, integrals and densities. *You will be able to* follow what
`loss.backward()` computes, and why a learning rate that is too large explodes.

**Parts 7 and 8, Probability and statistics.** Distributions, expectation, variance, the Gaussian, why the mean
minimizes squared error, covariance as a dot product, "why squares everywhere". *You will be able to* read a loss
function and say what assumption about the noise it encodes.

**Parts 9 and 10, Learning machines and attention.** Linear and logistic regression, a small network trained by hand,
embeddings, one attention head, a transformer block, LoRA. *You will be able to* read the diagram of a transformer
and point at the dot products.

**Advanced track and Branch Q.** SVD and PCA, rotations and RoPE, matrix calculus, optimizers, information theory,
normalization, floating point and quantization (FP16, FP8, NVFP4), flows. *You will be able to* choose a number format
for a model and know what you give up.

## Where it is used

- **Large language models**: embeddings, attention and feed-forward layers are all matrix products of the kind above;
  "KV cache", "quantization" and "LoRA" are each one lesson of this course applied to one of those products.
- **Image and video models**: convolutions are dot products of a small filter with every patch; diffusion models are
  gradient steps on a density (Parts 4, 7 and the advanced track).
- **Recommendation and search**: a query embedding dotted with every document embedding; nearest neighbours are the
  largest cosines (lesson 1.7).
- **Game and VFX ML tools**: denoisers, upscalers, pose estimation, motion matching, neural materials: tensors in,
  tensors out, the same layers, usually quantized to run on the GPU next to your renderer.
- **Running a model at all**: whether a model fits in your VRAM is a question about parameter count times bytes per
  number, which is Branch Q.

## Why learn it, rather than only use it

- To **read a paper** past the abstract: the equations become the same three or four pictures in different fonts.
- To **debug a model**: a shape error, a NaN, a loss that stalls: each is one of the lessons here failing in practice.
- To **choose formats and sizes**: FP16 versus FP8, rank of a LoRA, batch size: decisions that need the picture, not
  a rule of thumb.
- To **stop being afraid of formulas**. A formula is a compressed picture; the course shows the picture first, every
  time, with live numbers you can drag.

## How to use the course

- **Scenes** are live viewports in the style of a 3D app: drag the round handles, scrub numbers in the formula, type
  in the panel on the right (press N to toggle it), orbit 3D views by dragging empty space, Ctrl+wheel to zoom. The
  numbers in the scene, the formula, the table and the code line are the same numbers: hover one and the others light up.
- **Timelines** are animations you scrub, not videos: step forward and back, drag the slider, and the scene keeps
  every value you set.
- **Python cells** run in your browser (click Run; the first click loads the runtime) and open in Colab. The numbers
  they print are the numbers in the scene.
- **Maps**: every lesson ends with its local term map; the glossary and the roadmap are generated from the same graph.
  "Built on" at the top of each lesson tells you what to read first if something is unclear.
- **Mark a lesson done** at the bottom; progress is kept in your browser.

## Honest expectations

The lessons are short and the pictures are gentle, but the ideas still take time. Plan on roughly an hour per lesson
with the scenes, more for the ones marked as capstones. Nothing is skipped and nothing is hand-waved: when a lesson says
"this equals that", there is a scene where you can check it with your own numbers. If a lesson feels fast, drop one
level down its "Built on" links; that is what they are for.
