**Built on:** [A vector: an arrow and a list of numbers](lesson:vector)

A particle has velocity `v`. Every frame your solver does `v += gravity * dt`. A blend-shape node takes a base face and adds `0.5 * smile + 0.25 * jawOpen`. Both lines use the only two operations vectors have: add two arrows, and scale an arrow by a number. Everything else in this part of the course, and most of a neural network, is built from these two.

## 1. Adding: chain the walks

To add two vectors, walk the first, then walk the second from where you stopped. The sum is the arrow from where you started to where you ended. In components, that is just adding slot by slot:

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_x + b_x,\; a_y + b_y)
$$

Example. $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (3, -1)$:

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (1 + 3,\; 2 + (-1)) = (4, 1)
$$

Walk 1 right and 2 up, then 3 right and 1 down; you end at $(4, 1)$. Draw both orders, "a then b" and "b then a", and you get the two halves of a parallelogram; the sum is its diagonal. The order does not matter: $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}$.

Drag the tips of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. The two extra copies are the two walks, "a then b" and "b then a";
they meet at the same corner, the tip of the sum. Hover a number of the sum in the table and the pair it adds lights up.

**Misconception to drop:** "the length of $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$ is the length of $\mathbf{a}$ plus the length of $\mathbf{b}$". Only when the arrows point the same way. $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$ have lengths 3 and 4; their sum $(3, 4)$ has length 5, not 7. Walking east and then north is shorter than the sum of the two legs; that is Pythagoras again.

## 2. Scaling: same direction, new length

To scale a vector by a number $s$, multiply every component by $s$:

$$
s \, \mathbf{a} = (s\,a_x,\; s\,a_y)
$$

Example. $3 \cdot (2, -1) = (6, -3)$: three times as long, same direction. $0.5 \cdot (2, -1) = (1, -0.5)$: half as long. A plain number used this way is called a **scalar**, because it scales.

A negative scalar turns the arrow around: $-1 \cdot (2, -1) = (-2, 1)$ points the opposite way. Scaling never tilts an arrow. The only thing a negative sign can do to the direction is flip it.

Scrub $s$ in the formula (drag the number sideways) or in the panel. The arrow slides along one dashed line: it
stretches, shrinks, passes through the zero vector at $s = 0$ and comes out the other side for negative $s$.

## 3. Subtracting is adding the flipped arrow

$\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ means $\mathbf{a} + (-1)\,\mathbf{b}$: walk $\mathbf{a}$, then walk $\mathbf{b}$ backwards. With $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (3, -1)$:

$$
\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = (1 - 3,\; 2 - (-1)) = (-2, 3)
$$

Geometrically $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ is the arrow from the tip of $\mathbf{b}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{a}$, the same "destination minus start" rule as in [lesson 1.1](lesson:vector). The arrow from $\mathbf{a}$ to $\mathbf{b}$ is $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$.

**Misconception to drop:** "$\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ is the arrow from $\mathbf{a}$ to $\mathbf{b}$". It is the arrow from $\mathbf{b}$ to $\mathbf{a}$. The second letter is where you start.

## 4. Linear combinations

Scale a few vectors and add them up: that is a **linear combination**.

$$
s\,\mathbf{a} + t\,\mathbf{b}
$$

Example. With $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)$, $\mathbf{b} = (3, -1)$, $s = 2$, $t = -1$:

$$
2\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = (2, 4) + (-3, 1) = (-1, 5)
$$

Now scrub $s$ and $t$ freely and watch where the tip of $s\,\mathbf{a} + t\,\mathbf{b}$ lands. Stamp every point you reach. With two arrows that do not lie on one line, the stamps fill the whole plane: every point is some $s\,\mathbf{a} + t\,\mathbf{b}$. That filled region has a name, **span**, which [lesson 1.6](lesson:basis) gives it.

Below, $s\,\mathbf{a}$ is walked first and $t\,\mathbf{b}$ from its tip; the tip of the sum is $\mathbf{r}$. Scrub $s$
and $t$, then switch on **Paint** in the panel and scrub again: every tip you reach leaves a dot. Within the scrub range
the dots spread over a parallelogram, and larger $s$ and $t$ keep growing it in every direction. Then put $\mathbf{a}$ and
$\mathbf{b}$ on one line and paint once more.

A special case you already use every day. Take the two axis arrows $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$. Which $s$ and $t$ give $(3, 4)$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
&s \cdot (1, 0) + t \cdot (0, 1) = (s, t) = (3, 4) \\
&\Rightarrow\quad s = 3,\; t = 4
\end{aligned}
$$

The components of a vector are exactly its coefficients along the axis arrows. We will see this again as "coordinates are shadows" in [lesson 1.5](lesson:projection).

Coefficients can be any numbers: negative, zero, fractional. There is no rule that they must be non-negative or add up to 1 (both rules together, non-negative *and* adding up to 1, give a *convex* combination, a different thing).

In 3D a third arrow is needed to leave a plane. Below, $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ lie on the floor and
$\mathbf{c}$ points up; the result is $\mathbf{r} = s\,\mathbf{a} + t\,\mathbf{b} + u\,\mathbf{c}$, walked head to
tail. With $u = 0$ the walk never leaves the floor plane of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

## 5. Blend shapes are linear combinations

A blend shape target is stored as a **delta**: for each vertex, the vector from the base position to the target position. The deformer computes

$$
P' = P_0 + w_1\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_1 + w_2\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_2 + \cdots
$$

where $P_0$ is the base position of the vertex. That is a linear combination of the delta vectors, added to the base point. One vertex, two targets, real numbers: base $P_0 = (1.0, 0.5)$, smile delta $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_1 = (0.2, 0.3)$, jaw delta $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_2 = (0.0, -0.4)$, weights $w_1 = 0.5$, $w_2 = 0.25$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P' &= (1.0, 0.5) \\
&\quad + 0.5\,(0.2, 0.3) + 0.25\,(0.0, -0.4) \\
&= (1.0 + 0.1 + 0.0,\; 0.5 + 0.15 - 0.1) \\
&= (1.1, 0.55)
\end{aligned}
$$

Every vertex of the face does this with its own deltas and the same weights. The sliders you drag in the Shape Editor are the $w_i$.

Here is a face whose mouth has six vertices. Each vertex has its own smile delta and jaw delta; the two weights are
shared. Scrub $w_1$ and $w_2$: every vertex moves by its own $w_1\boldsymbol{\Delta}_1 + w_2\boldsymbol{\Delta}_2$ (the
two short colored segments), and the formula follows the right corner, the vertex of the example above. Try a negative
weight too.

The physics line is the same shape: `v += g * dt` is $\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v} + dt \, \mathbf{g}$. With $\mathbf{v} = (2, 0)$, $\mathbf{g} = (0, -9.8)$ and $dt = 0.1$: $\mathbf{v}' = (2, 0) + (0, -0.98) = (2, -0.98)$.

> **Say it simpler.** Adding = chaining the walks. Scaling = same direction, different length (negative = turn around). A linear combination = "some of this arrow plus some of that one".

## Exercises

1. $(1, 2) + (3, -1)$
2. $3 \cdot (2, -1)$
3. $2\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ for $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)$, $\mathbf{b} = (3, -1)$
4. Find $s$ and $t$ such that $s \cdot (1, 0) + t \cdot (0, 1) = (3, 4)$.
5. A vertex has base position $(0, 1, 0)$ and one delta $(0, 0.5, 0.2)$ at weight $0.4$. Where is it?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $(4, 1)$.
2. $(6, -3)$.
3. $(2, 4) - (3, -1) = (-1, 5)$.
4. $s = 3$, $t = 4$: the components are the coefficients along the axes.
5. $(0, 1, 0) + 0.4\,(0, 0.5, 0.2) = (0,\; 1.2,\; 0.08)$.

## Python

NumPy applies `+`, `-` and `*` by a scalar component by component, for arrays of any length. The code for 2 components and for 4096 is the same line.

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1., 2.])
b = np.array([3., -1.])
print(a + b)            # [4. 1.]
print(3 * np.array([2., -1.]))   # [ 6. -3.]
print(a - b)            # [-2.  3.]   from the tip of b to the tip of a

s, t = 2.0, -1.0
print(s * a + t * b)    # [-1.  5.]

# blend shape: base + sum of weight * delta, one vertex
base = np.array([1.0, 0.5])
deltas = np.array([[0.2, 0.3], [0.0, -0.4]])   # one row per target
w = np.array([0.5, 0.25])
print(base + w[0] * deltas[0] + w[1] * deltas[1])   # [1.1  0.55]

# the same thing works for 4096 components without changing the code
big = np.ones(4096)
print((2 * big + 3 * big)[:3])   # [5. 5. 5.]
```

</PyCell>

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: why adding vectors is cheap</summary>

A CPU does not add the components one after another. A SIMD instruction such as `_mm_add_ps` adds four `float32` lanes in one instruction; AVX does eight; a GPU `vec4` add is one instruction per thread. Adding two 4-component vectors is one instruction. Adding two vectors of a million components is a loop of about 125 000 AVX instructions, not a million. "Vectorized" NumPy code is fast because it hands this loop to compiled code that uses these lanes.

</details>
