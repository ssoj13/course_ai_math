Houdini computes a polygon's normal as `@N = normalize(cross(e1, e2))` from two edges. A shader builds the bitangent as `cross(N, T)`. A rasterizer decides front from back by the sign of a 2D cross product of the screen-space edges. Every one of these is the operation below.

## 1. Definition: the arrow that sticks out

The **cross product** of two 3D vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is a new 3D vector:

$$
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = (a_y b_z - a_z b_y,\;\; a_z b_x - a_x b_z,\;\; a_x b_y - a_y b_x)
$$

Each component is a small 2-by-2 "criss-cross": the x component uses only the y and z parts of the inputs, and so on, cycling x → y → z → x. Unlike the dot product, the result is a **vector**, and it exists only in 3D.

Example. $\mathbf{a} = (1, 0, 0)$, $\mathbf{b} = (0, 1, 0)$:

$$
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = (0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1,\;\; 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0,\;\; 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0) = (0, 0, 1)
$$

The x axis crossed with the y axis is the z axis. The result points straight out of the plane of the two inputs.

That is the general rule: $\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}$ is **perpendicular to both** $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. Check it with the dot product on a less obvious pair, $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2, 3)$, $\mathbf{b} = (4, 5, 6)$:

$$
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = (2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 5,\;\; 3 \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot 6,\;\; 1 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 4) = (-3, 6, -3)
$$

$(-3, 6, -3) \cdot (1, 2, 3) = -3 + 12 - 9 = 0$ and $(-3, 6, -3) \cdot (4, 5, 6) = -12 + 30 - 18 = 0$. Perpendicular to both, as promised.

Which of the two perpendicular directions? The formula always gives x × y = +z. The **right-hand rule** describes the picture in a right-handed system such as Maya or Houdini (y up, +z toward you): curl the fingers of your right hand from $\mathbf{a}$ toward $\mathbf{b}$ and the thumb points along $\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}$. In a left-handed system (Unity, Unreal, RenderMan) the same numbers are drawn with +z pointing away, so the picture follows the left hand; the arithmetic is unchanged.

## 2. Length: the area of the parallelogram

The length of the cross product is

$$
\lvert \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} \rvert = \lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert\,\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert \sin\theta
$$

Compare with the dot product's $\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert \cos\theta$. Cosine measures the shadow; sine measures the height. Base times height is an area, so $\lvert \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} \rvert$ is the **area of the parallelogram** spanned by $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

Check with the example: $\lvert (-3, 6, -3) \rvert = \sqrt{9 + 36 + 9} = \sqrt{54} \approx 7.35$. By the other route: $\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert = \sqrt{14}$, $\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert = \sqrt{77}$, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 32$, so $\cos\theta = 32 / \sqrt{14 \cdot 77} \approx 0.9746$, $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - 0.9746^2} \approx 0.224$, and $\sqrt{14 \cdot 77} \cdot 0.224 \approx 7.35$. Same number.

Two consequences:

- Parallel arrows give the **zero vector**. $(1, 2, 0) \times (2, 4, 0) = (0 - 0,\; 0 - 0,\; 4 - 4) = (0, 0, 0)$: a parallelogram with no height has no area. The cross product is not undefined here; it is zero, and that zero is the degenerate normal that makes `normalize` fail in [lesson 1.3](lesson:length).
- Perpendicular arrows give the biggest result, $\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert$ (since $\sin 90^\circ = 1$). For other angles the length is strictly smaller.

**Misconception to drop:** "$\lvert \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} \rvert = \lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert$ always." Only at 90°. In general there is a $\sin\theta$ factor, just as the dot product has a $\cos\theta$.

## 3. Order matters: $\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a} = -(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b})$

Swap the inputs and every criss-cross changes sign:

$$
\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a} = (b_y a_z - b_z a_y,\; \ldots) = -(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b})
$$

$(0, 1, 0) \times (1, 0, 0) = (0, 0, -1)$: y crossed with x is *minus* z. Same line, same area, opposite arrow. The dot product commutes ([lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product), checkpoint 2); the cross product anti-commutes. This single fact is the whole story of winding order in section 5.

## 4. The 2D cousin: signed area

In 2D there is no third axis to stick out along, so there is no cross product *vector*. What survives is the z component of the 3D formula applied to vectors with $z = 0$:

$$
a_x b_y - a_y b_x
$$

This number is the **signed area** of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. Example: $\mathbf{a} = (2, 0)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1, 3)$: $2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 1 = 6$. The parallelogram has base 2 and height 3; area 6.

The sign is the **orientation**: positive when turning from $\mathbf{a}$ to $\mathbf{b}$ is counter-clockwise, negative when clockwise, zero when they are on one line. $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 0)$ in the other order give $1 \cdot 0 - 3 \cdot 2 = -6$: same shape, clockwise turn, negative. This is the "dependence meter" of [lesson 1.6](lesson:basis): zero area means the two arrows span only a line.

{/* scene: 1.8-2 — 2D signed area a_x b_y − a_y b_x: positive when counter-clockwise, the sign shown as the parallelogram tint (orientation); hatched when negative */}

**Misconception to drop:** "the cross product exists in 2D as a vector." In 2D it is a single signed number; the vector version needs three dimensions.

## 5. Triangle normals and winding order

A triangle with vertices $P_0, P_1, P_2$ has two edge vectors from $P_0$: $\mathbf{e}_1 = P_1 - P_0$ and $\mathbf{e}_2 = P_2 - P_0$ ([lesson 1.1](lesson:vector)). Its normal is

$$
\mathbf{N} = \widehat{\mathbf{e}_1 \times \mathbf{e}_2}
$$

the cross product, normalized. Example: $P_0 = (0, 0, 0)$, $P_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, $P_2 = (0, 1, 0)$. Then $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{N} = (0, 0, 1)$: the triangle lies in the xy plane and faces $+z$. Its area is half the parallelogram, $\tfrac{1}{2} \lvert \mathbf{e}_1 \times \mathbf{e}_2 \rvert = 0.5$.

Now list the vertices in the other order, $P_0, P_2, P_1$. The edges swap, the cross product flips, and $\mathbf{N} = (0, 0, -1)$. The triangle did not move; only the **winding order** changed, and the normal now faces the other way. With back-face culling on, such a triangle simply disappears: the rasterizer drops it by the sign of its 2D signed area on screen (section 4). With two-sided display, it is drawn but lit with a flipped normal, and $\max(0, \mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L}) = 0$ turns the side that should be lit black. Both symptoms of a mesh with inconsistent winding come from this one sign.

{/* scene: 1.8-3 — triangle normal from two edges; flip the winding order and the normal flips: "this is why back-faces vanish or go dark" */}

The same logic builds tangent frames: given a normal $\mathbf{N}$ and a tangent $\mathbf{T}$, the bitangent is $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{N} \times \mathbf{T}$ (or $\mathbf{T} \times \mathbf{N}$, depending on the convention your normal maps were baked with; getting the order wrong mirrors every bump).

> **Say it simpler.** The cross product makes the arrow that sticks out of the plane of two arrows; its length is the area they span. Swap the two arrows and it points the other way.

## Exercises

1. $(1, 0, 0) \times (0, 1, 0)$
2. $(1, 2, 0) \times (2, 4, 0)$
3. The area of the parallelogram spanned by $(2, 0)$ and $(1, 3)$.
4. The unit normal of the triangle $(0, 0, 0), (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0)$, and the normal if the last two vertices are swapped.
5. $(1, 2, 3) \times (4, 5, 6)$, then check with a dot product that it is perpendicular to $(1, 2, 3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $(0, 0, 1)$.
2. $(0, 0, 0)$: the vectors are parallel.
3. $2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 1 = 6$.
4. $\mathbf{e}_1 \times \mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 0, 1)$, already unit. Swapped: $(0, 0, -1)$.
5. $(-3, 6, -3)$; $(-3)(1) + 6 \cdot 2 + (-3)(3) = -3 + 12 - 9 = 0$.

</details>

## Python

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1., 0., 0.]); b = np.array([0., 1., 0.])
print(np.cross(a, b))                  # [0. 0. 1.]
print(np.cross(b, a))                  # [ 0.  0. -1.]   order flips the result

p = np.array([1., 2., 3.]); q = np.array([4., 5., 6.])
c = np.cross(p, q)
print(c, c @ p, c @ q)                 # [-3.  6. -3.] 0.0 0.0   perpendicular to both
print(round(np.linalg.norm(c), 4))     # 7.3485  = parallelogram area

print(np.cross([1., 2., 0.], [2., 4., 0.]))   # [0. 0. 0.]  parallel

# 2D signed area
u = np.array([2., 0.]); v = np.array([1., 3.])
print(u[0]*v[1] - u[1]*v[0])           # 6.0   counter-clockwise
print(v[0]*u[1] - v[1]*u[0])           # -6.0  clockwise

# triangle normal and winding
P0, P1, P2 = np.zeros(3), np.array([1., 0., 0.]), np.array([0., 1., 0.])
n = np.cross(P1 - P0, P2 - P0)
print(n / np.linalg.norm(n))           # [0. 0. 1.]
n = np.cross(P2 - P0, P1 - P0)         # vertices listed the other way round
print(n / np.linalg.norm(n))           # [ 0.  0. -1.]
```

</PyCell>

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: six multiplies, and a volume</summary>

A cross product is 6 multiplications and 3 subtractions, no square root; it is cheaper than a normalize. One more identity to carry into Part 2: $(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot \mathbf{c}$ is the signed **volume** of the box spanned by three vectors (area of the base times the height along the normal). Its sign says whether the three vectors form a right-handed or left-handed frame. Part 2 will call this number the determinant.

</details>
