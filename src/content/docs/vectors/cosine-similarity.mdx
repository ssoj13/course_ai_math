Smooth shading has a crease angle: two faces share a soft edge when their normals are within 30°, a hard edge otherwise. The test is not an angle at all; for unit normals it is `dot(N1, N2) > cos(30°)`. An asset library that finds "similar" textures does the same test on much longer vectors. So does every AI system that finds "similar" words, documents or images. This lesson is that one test, named, and then a tour of where AI uses it.

## 1. Cosine similarity: direction only

From [lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product), $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert \cos\theta$. Divide the lengths away:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert\,\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert} = \hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}}
$$

This number is the **cosine similarity** of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. It is the dot product of the two normalized vectors ([lesson 1.3](lesson:length)), so it only sees the directions. Its range is the range of cosine:

- $1$: the arrows point the same way.
- $0$: perpendicular; in embedding language, "unrelated".
- $-1$: opposite directions.

Example 1. $\mathbf{a} = (1, 1)$, $\mathbf{b} = (2, 2)$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 4$, $\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert = \sqrt 2$, $\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert = 2\sqrt 2$, so $\cos\theta = 4 / (\sqrt 2 \cdot 2 \sqrt 2) = 4/4 = 1$. Same direction, different length: similarity 1.

Example 2. $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$, similarity 0.

Example 3. $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 2 + 2 = 4$, both lengths $\sqrt 5$, so $\cos\theta = 4/5 = 0.8$. Similar but not identical ($\theta \approx 37^\circ$).

One rule before we go on: the **zero vector** $(0, 0)$ has no direction. The formula would divide by its length, 0, so its cosine similarity with anything is *undefined* (not 0). Every scene below shows a dash in that case.

A toy picture, hand-made, not a real embedding: give every word two made-up features, "furry" and "has wheels". Cat $= (0.9, 0.1)$, dog $= (0.8, 0.2)$, car $= (0.1, 0.9)$. For cat and dog the dot product is $0.72 + 0.02 = 0.74$ and the lengths are $0.906$ and $0.825$; for cat and car the dot product is $0.09 + 0.09 = 0.18$ and both lengths are $0.906$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{0.74}{0.906 \cdot 0.825} &\approx 0.99 \\
\frac{0.18}{0.906 \cdot 0.906} &\approx 0.22
\end{aligned}
$$

Cat and dog point almost the same way; cat and car do not. Drag the tips of the three arrows (or type in the panel): the arcs show the two angles, and the panel shows all three similarities.

## 2. What cosine ignores: length

Scale one vector and the similarity does not move. $(3, 4)$ and $(6, 8)$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 18 + 32 = 50$, lengths $5$ and $10$, similarity $50/50 = 1$. Compare with $(3, 4)$ and $(4, 3)$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 12 + 12 = 24$, both lengths $5$, similarity $24/25 = 0.96$.

Euclidean distance ([lesson 0.3](lesson:pythagoras)) sees the opposite picture. $(3, 4)$ to $(6, 8)$ is $\lvert (-3, -4) \rvert = 5$ apart; $(3, 4)$ to $(4, 3)$ is $\lvert (-1, 1) \rvert = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.41$ apart. By distance, $(4, 3)$ is the closer one; by cosine, $(6, 8)$ is the perfect match. Neither is wrong; they measure different things. Cosine asks "same direction?", distance asks "same place?".

In the scene, $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$ and the second arrow is $s \cdot \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b} = (4, 3)$. Drag its tip along the dashed line (or scrub $s$ in the formula): the cosine stays at $0.960$ while the distance between the tips changes. At $s = 0$ the arrow shrinks to the zero vector and the cosine is undefined.

**Misconception to drop:** "cosine similarity is a distance." It is not: it grows when things get *closer* in direction, it ignores length entirely, and it can be negative. People sometimes use $1 - \cos\theta$ as a "cosine distance", which at least points the right way.

**Misconception to drop:** "scaling an embedding changes its similarities." Multiply a vector by any positive number and every cosine similarity with it is unchanged.

## 3. Embeddings: meaning as an arrow

An **embedding** is a vector that stands for something that is not a number: a word, a token, an image, an audio clip. A model learns the components so that things used in similar ways end up pointing in similar directions. No component has a name like "furry"; only the geometry means something, and the geometry is read with cosine similarity.

### The embedding table an LLM starts with

A language model never sees letters. Its first step is a **tokenizer** that cuts the text into pieces and replaces each piece by an integer, its **token id**. Its second step is a lookup in the **embedding table**: a big grid of numbers with one row per token id. Token id 5 451 means "copy row 5 451". That is the whole operation; no arithmetic happens, exactly like reading texel $(u, v)$ from a texture by integer coordinates.

The table below is real. It is a slice of **GloVe** (Stanford, 2014), a classic table of 400 001 rows (one padding row plus 400 000 words), 300 numbers per row, learned from Wikipedia and newswire text. The rows are sorted by how often the word occurs, so "the" is row 1 and "cat" is row 5 451. Click a word of the sentence, or a row of the heatmap:

Every row is a 300-dimensional arrow. The same idea at full scale: a modern LLM's table has around 150 000 rows of a few thousand numbers each, and the rows are learned together with the rest of the model.

Concrete numbers from this table: `cat` and `dog` have cosine $0.68$, `cat` and `car` $0.15$, `king` and `queen` $0.63$. Three cautions that matter in practice:

- **Opposite meaning is not opposite direction.** `hot` and `cold` have cosine $0.51$, `good` and `bad` $0.65$: antonyms are used in the same sentences ("the water is hot / cold"), so they point in similar directions. Of the 1 891 pairs in this table only 150 have a negative cosine, and the most negative is just $-0.19$. Treat negative values as "far", not "anti".
- **Random vectors in high dimension are almost always nearly perpendicular.** With 300 random components the cosine between two vectors is typically within about $\pm 0.06$ of 0 (roughly $1/\sqrt{300}$); with 4 096 components, within about $\pm 0.016$. So $0.3$ is far above chance in 300-D, while in 2D two random directions show $0.3$ all the time. The baseline also depends on the model: many trained spaces have all cosines shifted positive, as here, so compare against typical values for that model, not against 0. Part 10 uses the random-vector fact to explain the $\sqrt d$ in attention.
- **The picture is not the space.** Every 2D or 3D map of embeddings is a projection; judge similarity by the number, not by the drawing.

### Nearest neighbours

The most common question asked of an embedding table: which rows are most similar to this one? Compute the cosine with every row and sort. Below, the search runs over the 62 table words, live. This "sort by cosine" is also how **recommendation** works: a film or a product gets an embedding, you get an embedding from what you watched or bought, and the nearest rows are what you are shown next.

### Analogies: arithmetic on arrows, and where it breaks

Directions can carry relations. The arrow from `man` to `woman` (the difference `woman − man`) is roughly the same as the arrow from `king` to `queen`. So start at `king`, subtract `man`, add `woman`, and look for the nearest word: **king − man + woman ≈ ?** The arithmetic is plain vector arithmetic from [lesson 1.2](lesson:vector-arithmetic), done on all 300 components at once.

In GloVe the nearest word (inputs excluded) is "queen", with cosine $0.69$. Try the examples, then build your own with the three selectors. Read the verdict under each example: several famous-looking results only work because the three input words are dropped from the answers, and some relations do not survive the arithmetic at all.

<CosAnalogy locale={locale} client:load />

What to take away: analogy arithmetic works for relations the training text repeats thousands of times (gender of family words, capital of a country) and fails quietly elsewhere, because nearest-neighbour search always returns *something*. It is a demonstration of the geometry, not a reasoning engine.

### Dimension: hundreds to thousands of numbers

How long are these arrows in practice? GloVe uses 300 numbers per word. The search model of the next section uses 384 numbers per text. The token table of the 1.7-billion-parameter LLM above uses 2 048, and large models use 4 096 to over 12 000. In [lesson 1.6](lesson:basis) terms, the dimension is just the count of slots in each row: more slots let the model keep more distinctions apart, at a cost in memory and compute that grows with every row.

## 4. Semantic search and RAG

An embedding model does not have to stop at single words. A **text embedding model** reads a whole sentence or paragraph and returns one vector for it, trained so that a question and a passage that answers it point in similar directions, even when they share no words.

**Semantic search** is then nearest neighbours again: embed every document once and store the vectors (this store is called a **vector database**); embed the question when it arrives; rank the documents by cosine; return the top $k$. Below, 12 short passages and 5 questions were embedded by a real model (snowflake-arctic-embed-xs, 384 numbers per text). Your browser only computes the cosines and sorts. Notice that "How do I make a loaf at home?" finds the passage about dough and baking, although "loaf" appears nowhere in it.

<CosSearch locale={locale} client:load />

**RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation) is this search plus one more step: the top passages are pasted into the prompt of a language model, which answers from them. The language model does no searching itself; the cosine ranking decides what it gets to read. When a chatbot answers from your company's documents, this is usually what happens behind it.

## 5. All pairs at once

With $n$ embeddings you often want the similarity of every pair (the heatmap above is such a table). First normalize every vector; then each similarity is a plain dot product, no divisions left. The explicit loop, on the toy table of section 1:

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

E = np.array([[0.9, 0.1], [0.8, 0.2], [0.1, 0.9]])   # cat, dog, car (toy)
E_hat = E / np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)  # normalize every row
n = len(E_hat)
S = np.empty((n, n))
for i in range(n):
    for j in range(n):
        S[i, j] = E_hat[i] @ E_hat[j]
print(np.round(S, 2))
# [[1.   0.99 0.22]
#  [0.99 1.   0.35]
#  [0.22 0.35 1.  ]]
```

</PyCell>

NumPy writes the same loop as `S = E_hat @ E_hat.T`. **This `@` between two matrices is Part 2 material.** For now read it as exactly the double loop above: every row of the first dotted with every row of the second, all at once. It is shown here only so the Python cell matches what you will see in real code.

## 6. Preview: attention compares embeddings with dot products

The central operation of a transformer, **attention**, starts with exactly the table of section 5: for a sentence of $n$ tokens it computes the dot product of every token's vector with every other token's vector and uses the scores to decide which tokens each token should "look at". Here is that raw table for "the cat sat on the mat", using the GloVe rows directly:

<CosAttention locale={locale} client:load />

Two things are missing that the real mechanism adds, and both are Part 10: the vectors are first multiplied by learned matrices (so "cat" can look for its verb rather than for itself, which is why the diagonal dominates here), and the scores are divided by $\sqrt d$ and turned into weights that sum to 1. The comparison itself is the dot product you already know.

> **Say it simpler.** Cosine similarity ignores length and keeps only direction: 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated, −1 = opposite. An embedding is an arrow that stands for a word or a text; an AI system finds "similar" things by finding arrows that point the same way.

## Exercises

1. Cosine similarity of $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$.
2. Of $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$.
3. Of $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$.
4. Which pair is more similar by cosine: $(3, 4), (4, 3)$ or $(3, 4), (6, 8)$? And which pair is closer by Euclidean distance?
5. Of $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $1$ (same direction).
2. $0$ (perpendicular).
3. $4 / (\sqrt 5 \cdot \sqrt 5) = 0.8$.
4. By cosine: $(3, 4), (6, 8)$ with similarity $1$ beats $0.96$. By distance: $(3, 4), (4, 3)$ at $\sqrt 2$ beats $5$.
5. Undefined: the zero vector has no direction, and the formula would divide by $0$.

</details>

## Python

The rounded prints hide the `0.9999999999999998` that [lesson 1.3](lesson:length) warned about; remove the `round` to see it.

<PyCell rows={10}>

```python
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    return (a @ b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

print(round(cosine(np.array([1., 1.]), np.array([2., 2.])), 6))   # 1.0
print(round(cosine(np.array([1., 0.]), np.array([0., 1.])), 6))   # 0.0
print(round(cosine(np.array([1., 2.]), np.array([2., 1.])), 6))   # 0.8

a = np.array([3., 4.])
print(round(cosine(a, np.array([6., 8.])), 6), round(cosine(a, np.array([4., 3.])), 6))   # 1.0 0.96
print(np.linalg.norm(a - [6., 8.]), round(np.linalg.norm(a - [4., 3.]), 6))              # 5.0 1.414214

# all pairs: normalize rows, then every similarity is one dot product
E = np.array([[0.9, 0.1], [0.8, 0.2], [0.1, 0.9]])   # cat, dog, car (toy)
E_hat = E / np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)
S = np.empty((3, 3))
for i in range(3):
    for j in range(3):
        S[i, j] = E_hat[i] @ E_hat[j]
print(np.round(S, 2))
print(np.allclose(S, E_hat @ E_hat.T))   # True: the matrix product is the same double loop (Part 2)

# random high-dimensional vectors are nearly perpendicular
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x, y = rng.standard_normal(4096), rng.standard_normal(4096)
print(round(cosine(x, y), 3))            # close to 0: a few hundredths at most
```

</PyCell>

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: where embeddings live</summary>

An embedding table is a matrix with one row per token; looking a token up is picking a row by index, no arithmetic. Search systems store the rows already normalized, so a similarity query is one dot product per row, and they often quantize the components to `int8` or `fp16` to fit more rows in memory, accepting a small error in each cosine. That trade is the subject of the number-formats branch at the end of the course. Even this page makes the trade on a small scale: its GloVe rows are rounded to 3 decimals, which changes no cosine by more than 0.0003.

With millions of rows, comparing the query with every row is too slow, so vector databases use *approximate* nearest-neighbour indexes (graphs or clusters of rows) that check only a small fraction of the rows and occasionally miss the true best match.

</details>

## What this opens

Every tool in Part 1 acts on one arrow, or compares two. A model acts on *all* arrows at once, turning every vector in a space by the same rule: the learned matrices that attention multiplies its embeddings by are such rules. That rule is a matrix, and it is where Part 2 begins. (The side branch [The cross product](lesson:cross-product) can be read before or after.)
