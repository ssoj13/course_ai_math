**Built on:** [The dot product](lesson:dot-product) · [Length, Σ notation and normalization](lesson:length)

A character walks into a wall. The collision code does not stop him; it removes the part of his velocity that points into the wall and keeps the part that slides along it. A ray bounces off a mirror: `r = d - 2 * dot(d, n) * n`. Both lines split a vector into "the part along a direction" and "the rest". That split is projection, and the dot product of [lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product) computes it.

## 1. The length of a shadow

Take an arrow $\mathbf{a}$ and a direction $\mathbf{b}$. Shine a light from directly above the line of $\mathbf{b}$. The shadow of $\mathbf{a}$ on that line has length

$$
\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \cos\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ ([lesson 0.4](lesson:unit-circle): cosine is the shadow of a length-1 stick; here the stick has length $\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert$). From the dot product, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert \cos\theta$, so

$$
\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert} = \mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{b} / \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert$ is the unit vector along $\mathbf{b}$ ([lesson 1.3](lesson:length)). **The dot product with a unit vector is the length of the shadow on it.** This number is called the **scalar projection** of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$.

Example 1. $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1, 0)$. Here $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = (1, 0)$ already, and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0 = 3$. The shadow of $(3, 4)$ on the x axis is 3 long.

Example 2. $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1, 1)$. $\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert = \sqrt 2$, $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = (0.707, 0.707)$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} &= 3 \cdot 0.707 + 4 \cdot 0.707 \\
&= 7 \cdot 0.707 \approx 4.95
\end{aligned}
$$

The shadow is signed. If $\mathbf{a}$ leans backwards relative to $\mathbf{b}$ (angle past 90°) the cosine is negative and the shadow length is negative: it lies behind the start of $\mathbf{b}$.

Drag the tip of $\mathbf{a}$ around $\mathbf{b}$. The light falls straight onto the dashed line of $\mathbf{b}$; the band
from the origin to the foot of the light ray is the shadow, and its signed length is $\mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}}$.
Past 90° the foot moves behind the origin and the number turns negative; at exactly 90° there is no shadow at all.
Hover the shadow, its formula term or its panel row: all three light up together.

**The one axis that fails.** If $\mathbf{b}$ is the zero vector, there is no line to cast a shadow on: $\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert = 0$
and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} / \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert$ divides by zero. Drag $\mathbf{b}$ onto the origin to see it. The
dot product $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$ is still defined; the shadow is not.

**Misconception to drop:** "the shadow depends on how long $\mathbf{b}$ is." It does not. Scaling $\mathbf{b}$ by 10 changes $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ by 10 and $\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert$ by 10; the ratio, the shadow, is unchanged. Only the *direction* of $\mathbf{b}$ matters, which is why the formula uses $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$.

## 2. The vector projection and the leftover

The shadow as an arrow, not a number, is the scalar projection times the unit direction:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}})\, \hat{\mathbf{b}}
$$

What is left after you remove the shadow is the **perpendicular part**:

$$
\mathbf{perp} = \mathbf{a} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \mathbf{a}
$$

Example. $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$ onto $\mathbf{b} = (1, 0)$: $\mathbf{proj} = 3 \cdot (1, 0) = (3, 0)$ and $\mathbf{perp} = (3, 4) - (3, 0) = (0, 4)$. Check that perp really is perpendicular to $\mathbf{b}$: $(0, 4) \cdot (1, 0) = 0$. Yes.

Example with the diagonal. $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$ onto $\mathbf{b} = (1, 1)$: scalar projection $4.95$, so $\mathbf{proj} = 4.95 \cdot (0.707, 0.707) = (3.5, 3.5)$ and $\mathbf{perp} = (3, 4) - (3.5, 3.5) = (-0.5, 0.5)$. Check: $(-0.5, 0.5) \cdot (1, 1) = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0$. Perpendicular.

The two parts form a right triangle with $\mathbf{a}$ as the hypotenuse, so Pythagoras holds once more:

$$
\lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert^2 = \lvert \mathbf{proj} \rvert^2 + \lvert \mathbf{perp} \rvert^2
$$

With the diagonal example: $3.5^2 + 3.5^2 = 24.5$, plus $0.5^2 + 0.5^2 = 0.5$, total $25 = \lvert (3, 4) \rvert^2$.

Below, the same $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ (the two scenes share them) split into the shadow arrow $\mathbf{proj}$ and the
remainder $\mathbf{perp}$. The right angle at the foot is always there, and so are the three squares: the two small
ones always add up to the big one. The formula computes $\mathbf{proj}$ both ways, with $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ and without,
and the two results agree.

Because $\lvert \cos\theta \rvert \le 1$, the shadow is never longer than the arrow: $\lvert \mathbf{proj} \rvert \le \lvert \mathbf{a} \rvert$, with equality only when $\mathbf{a}$ already lies on the line of $\mathbf{b}$ (in either direction).

**If $\mathbf{b}$ is not normalized.** You can skip the normalization by dividing twice:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}}\; \mathbf{b}
$$

Same vector: one $\lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert$ cancels the length of $\mathbf{b}$ in the dot product, the other cancels the length of $\mathbf{b}$ itself ($\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lvert \mathbf{b} \rvert^2$, checkpoint 1 of [lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product)). Check: $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1, 1)$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 7$, $\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 2$, $\tfrac{7}{2}(1, 1) = (3.5, 3.5)$. Same as before.

**Misconception to drop:** "the scalar projection is a vector." It is one number, the signed shadow length. The vector projection is that number times $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$.

## 3. Coordinates are shadows

Now the small discovery this lesson is really about. Project $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$ onto the two axis arrows $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)$. Both are already unit vectors, so the shadows are plain dot products:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{e}_1 &= 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0 = 3 \\
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{e}_2 &= 3 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1 = 4
\end{aligned}
$$

The shadows are $3$ and $4$: the components of $\mathbf{a}$. The x coordinate of a vector *is* its shadow on the x axis. The dashed legs we drew in [lesson 1.1](lesson:vector) were projections all along, and the "coefficients along the axes" of [lesson 1.2](lesson:vector-arithmetic) are the same numbers once more:

$$
\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{e}_1)\, \mathbf{e}_1 + (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{e}_2)\, \mathbf{e}_2
$$

In the scene the two axes start as $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ (named $\mathbf{u}_1$, $\mathbf{u}_2$ because you can move
them). The table puts each shadow next to the coordinate along the same axis: $3 = 3$ and $4 = 4$. Turn the pair by
dragging the tip of $\mathbf{u}_1$: both shadows change, both still equal the coordinates, and adding them back still
lands exactly on $\mathbf{a}$.

Now drag the tip of $\mathbf{u}_2$ so the axes are no longer perpendicular. The shadows are still dot products, but they
are no longer the coordinates: the true coordinates (the solid path along $\mathbf{u}_1$, then parallel to
$\mathbf{u}_2$) are different numbers, and the arrow rebuilt from the shadows misses $\mathbf{a}$.

This is the sentence to remember from Part 1: **when the axes are perpendicular unit arrows, a coordinate is a dot
product with an axis. Only then.** Unit length matters too: with an axis of length 2 the dot product doubles, but the
coordinate along it halves. The next lesson asks which axes are allowed at all, and the general case (axes at any
angle, of any length) needs a tool from Part 2: undoing a matrix.

## 4. 3D: a shadow on a plane is two shadows

In 3D, "project onto the ground plane" means: remove the part along the plane's normal $\mathbf{n}$ (a unit vector) and keep the rest.

$$
\mathbf{a}_{\text{ground}} = \mathbf{a} - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{n})\, \mathbf{n}
$$

This is the collision line from the hook. Velocity $\mathbf{v} = (2, -3, 1)$, floor normal $\mathbf{n} = (0, 1, 0)$: $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{n} = -3$, so $\mathbf{v}_{\text{slide}} = (2, -3, 1) - (-3)(0, 1, 0) = (2, 0, 1)$. The downward part is gone; the sliding part stays.

Reflection removes that part *twice* (once to reach the surface, once more to bounce back out):

$$
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{d} - 2\,(\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{n})\, \mathbf{n}
$$

Incoming $\mathbf{d} = (1, -1, 0)$, mirror normal $\mathbf{n} = (0, 1, 0)$: $\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{n} = -1$, so $\mathbf{r} = (1, -1, 0) - 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot (0, 1, 0) = (1, 1, 0)$. Down-and-right became up-and-right. Both formulas need $\mathbf{n}$ to be a unit vector; otherwise the "$\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{n}$" denominator from section 2 must come back.

The scene starts with exactly this velocity and the floor. Drag the tip of $\mathbf{n}$ to tilt the plane (it stays a
unit vector), or drag $\mathbf{a}$ (PageUp/PageDown move it up and down). The shadow on the plane is the arrow
$\mathbf{a} - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{n})\,\mathbf{n}$, and the dashed path along the two in-plane axes $\mathbf{e}_1$,
$\mathbf{e}_2$ shows the title of this section: it is also two shadows added up,
$(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{e}_1)\,\mathbf{e}_1 + (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{e}_2)\,\mathbf{e}_2$, because $\mathbf{e}_1$,
$\mathbf{e}_2$ and $\mathbf{n}$ are three perpendicular unit arrows. Turn on **Reflection** in Overlays to see the bounce.

> **Say it simpler.** The dot product with a unit arrow is the length of the shadow on it. Coordinates are shadows on perpendicular unit axes. "Along" plus "across" rebuilds the original arrow.

## Exercises

1. Project $(3, 4)$ onto $(1, 0)$: scalar and vector projection.
2. Project $(3, 4)$ onto the unit vector $(1, 1)/\sqrt 2$.
3. The perpendicular remainder of $(3, 4)$ with respect to $(1, 0)$.
4. Scalar versus vector projection of $(2, 1)$ onto $(0, 1)$.
5. Reflect $\mathbf{d} = (1, -1, 0)$ off a surface with unit normal $\mathbf{n} = (0, 1, 0)$.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Scalar $3$; vector $(3, 0)$.
2. Scalar $7/\sqrt 2 \approx 4.95$; vector $(3.5, 3.5)$.
3. $(3, 4) - (3, 0) = (0, 4)$.
4. Scalar $1$ (the y component); vector $(0, 1)$.
5. $\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{n} = -1$; $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{d} - 2(-1)\,\mathbf{n} = (1, -1, 0) + (0, 2, 0) = (1, 1, 0)$.

</details>

## Python

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3., 4.])
b = np.array([1., 1.])
bh = b / np.linalg.norm(b)          # unit direction

scalar = a @ bh                      # length of the shadow
proj = scalar * bh                   # the shadow as a vector
perp = a - proj                      # the leftover
print(round(scalar, 4))              # 4.9497  (= 7 / sqrt 2)
print(proj)                          # [3.5 3.5]
print(perp)                          # [-0.5  0.5]
print(round(proj @ perp, 12))        # 0.0  (up to rounding): perpendicular
print((a @ b) / (b @ b) * b)         # [3.5 3.5]   same, without normalizing b

# coordinates are shadows on the axes
e1, e2 = np.array([1., 0.]), np.array([0., 1.])
print(a @ e1, a @ e2)                # 3.0 4.0

# slide along the floor, and reflect
v = np.array([2., -3., 1.]); n = np.array([0., 1., 0.])
print(v - (v @ n) * n)               # [2. 0. 1.]
d = np.array([1., -1., 0.])
print(d - 2 * (d @ n) * n)           # [1. 1. 0.]
```

</PyCell>

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: many shadows at once</summary>

Projecting one vector onto $k$ unit directions is $k$ dot products with the same input. Stack the $k$ directions as rows and the whole thing is one matrix–vector product (a GEMV in BLAS). That is the first honest glimpse of what a matrix is in this course: a bundle of dot products applied together. Part 2 makes it precise.

## What this opens

We projected onto the x and y axes and got the coordinates back. A tilted pair of perpendicular axes, $(1, 1)/\sqrt 2$ and $(1, -1)/\sqrt 2$, would give two different numbers that still rebuild the arrow; and axes that are not perpendicular would not work this way at all. So which sets of axes are allowed? [Basis, span, independence and dimension](lesson:basis).
