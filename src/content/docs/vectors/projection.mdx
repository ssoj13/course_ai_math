A character walks into a wall. The collision code does not stop him; it removes the part of his velocity that points into the wall and keeps the part that slides along it. A ray bounces off a mirror: `r = d - 2 * dot(d, n) * n`. Both lines split a vector into "the part along a direction" and "the rest". That split is projection, and the dot product of [lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product) computes it.

## 1. The length of a shadow

Take an arrow $a$ and a direction $b$. Shine a light from directly above the line of $b$. The shadow of $a$ on that line has length

$$
\lvert a \rvert \cos\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $a$ and $b$ ([lesson 0.4](lesson:unit-circle): cosine is the shadow of a length-1 stick; here the stick has length $\lvert a \rvert$). From the dot product, $a \cdot b = \lvert a \rvert \lvert b \rvert \cos\theta$, so

$$
\lvert a \rvert \cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lvert b \rvert} = a \cdot \hat{b}
$$

where $\hat{b} = b / \lvert b \rvert$ is the unit vector along $b$ ([lesson 1.3](lesson:length)). **The dot product with a unit vector is the length of the shadow on it.** This number is called the **scalar projection** of $a$ onto $b$.

Example 1. $a = (3, 4)$, $b = (1, 0)$. Here $\hat{b} = (1, 0)$ already, and $a \cdot \hat{b} = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0 = 3$. The shadow of $(3, 4)$ on the x axis is 3 long.

Example 2. $a = (3, 4)$, $b = (1, 1)$. $\lvert b \rvert = \sqrt 2$, $\hat{b} = (0.707, 0.707)$, and

$$
a \cdot \hat{b} = 3 \cdot 0.707 + 4 \cdot 0.707 = 7 \cdot 0.707 \approx 4.95
$$

The shadow is signed. If $a$ leans backwards relative to $b$ (angle past 90°) the cosine is negative and the shadow length is negative: it lies behind the start of $b$.

{/* scene: 1.5-1 — light from above the line of b; drag a, the shadow slides along b's line, goes negative past 90°; the term a·b̂ and the shadow segment share a color */}

**Misconception to drop:** "the shadow depends on how long $b$ is." It does not. Scaling $b$ by 10 changes $a \cdot b$ by 10 and $\lvert b \rvert$ by 10; the ratio, the shadow, is unchanged. Only the *direction* of $b$ matters, which is why the formula uses $\hat{b}$.

## 2. The vector projection and the leftover

The shadow as an arrow, not a number, is the scalar projection times the unit direction:

$$
\text{proj}_b\, a = (a \cdot \hat{b})\, \hat{b}
$$

What is left after you remove the shadow is the **perpendicular part**:

$$
\text{perp} = a - \text{proj}_b\, a
$$

Example. $a = (3, 4)$ onto $b = (1, 0)$: $\text{proj} = 3 \cdot (1, 0) = (3, 0)$ and $\text{perp} = (3, 4) - (3, 0) = (0, 4)$. Check that perp really is perpendicular to $b$: $(0, 4) \cdot (1, 0) = 0$. Yes.

Example with the diagonal. $a = (3, 4)$ onto $b = (1, 1)$: scalar projection $4.95$, so $\text{proj} = 4.95 \cdot (0.707, 0.707) = (3.5, 3.5)$ and $\text{perp} = (3, 4) - (3.5, 3.5) = (-0.5, 0.5)$. Check: $(-0.5, 0.5) \cdot (1, 1) = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0$. Perpendicular.

The two parts form a right triangle with $a$ as the hypotenuse, so Pythagoras holds once more:

$$
\lvert a \rvert^2 = \lvert \text{proj} \rvert^2 + \lvert \text{perp} \rvert^2
$$

With the diagonal example: $3.5^2 + 3.5^2 = 24.5$, plus $0.5^2 + 0.5^2 = 0.5$, total $25 = \lvert (3, 4) \rvert^2$.

{/* scene: 1.5-2 — the vector projection (a·b̂) b̂ and the leftover a − proj with the right angle marked; Pythagoras tiles |a|² = |proj|² + |perp|² */}

Because $\lvert \cos\theta \rvert \le 1$, the shadow is never longer than the arrow: $\lvert \text{proj} \rvert \le \lvert a \rvert$, with equality only when $a$ already lies along $b$.

**If $b$ is not normalized.** You can skip the normalization by dividing twice:

$$
\text{proj}_b\, a = \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}\; b
$$

Same vector: one $\lvert b \rvert$ cancels the length of $b$ in the dot product, the other cancels the length of $b$ itself ($b \cdot b = \lvert b \rvert^2$, checkpoint 1 of [lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product)). Check: $a = (3, 4)$, $b = (1, 1)$: $a \cdot b = 7$, $b \cdot b = 2$, $\tfrac{7}{2}(1, 1) = (3.5, 3.5)$. Same as before.

**Misconception to drop:** "the scalar projection is a vector." It is one number, the signed shadow length. The vector projection is that number times $\hat{b}$.

## 3. Coordinates are shadows

Now the small discovery this lesson is really about. Project $a = (3, 4)$ onto the two axis arrows $e_1 = (1, 0)$ and $e_2 = (0, 1)$. Both are already unit vectors, so the shadows are plain dot products:

$$
a \cdot e_1 = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0 = 3, \qquad a \cdot e_2 = 3 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1 = 4
$$

The shadows are $3$ and $4$: the components of $a$. The x coordinate of a vector *is* its shadow on the x axis. The dashed legs we drew in [lesson 1.1](lesson:vector) were projections all along, and the "coefficients along the axes" of [lesson 1.2](lesson:vector-arithmetic) are the same numbers once more:

$$
a = (a \cdot e_1)\, e_1 + (a \cdot e_2)\, e_2
$$

{/* scene: 1.5-3 — project a onto e₁ and e₂: the shadows are exactly aₓ and a_y; the table reads a·e₁ = aₓ */}

This is the sentence to remember from Part 1: **a coordinate is a dot product with a unit axis.** The next lesson asks which axes are allowed, and this sentence is what makes the answer work.

## 4. 3D: a shadow on a plane is two shadows

{/* scene: 1.5-4 — 3D: the shadow of a onto a plane is two projections (onto two perpendicular unit directions in the plane) plus the leftover along the normal */}

In 3D, "project onto the ground plane" means: remove the part along the plane's normal $n$ (a unit vector) and keep the rest.

$$
a_{\text{ground}} = a - (a \cdot n)\, n
$$

This is the collision line from the hook. Velocity $v = (2, -3, 1)$, floor normal $n = (0, 1, 0)$: $v \cdot n = -3$, so $v_{\text{slide}} = (2, -3, 1) - (-3)(0, 1, 0) = (2, 0, 1)$. The downward part is gone; the sliding part stays.

Reflection removes that part *twice* (once to reach the surface, once more to bounce back out):

$$
r = d - 2\,(d \cdot n)\, n
$$

Incoming $d = (1, -1, 0)$, mirror normal $n = (0, 1, 0)$: $d \cdot n = -1$, so $r = (1, -1, 0) - 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot (0, 1, 0) = (1, 1, 0)$. Down-and-right became up-and-right. Both formulas need $n$ to be a unit vector; otherwise the "$n \cdot n$" denominator from section 2 must come back.

> **Say it simpler.** The dot product with a unit arrow is the length of the shadow on it. Coordinates are shadows on the axes. "Along" plus "across" rebuilds the original arrow.

## Exercises

1. Project $(3, 4)$ onto $(1, 0)$: scalar and vector projection.
2. Project $(3, 4)$ onto the unit vector $(1, 1)/\sqrt 2$.
3. The perpendicular remainder of $(3, 4)$ with respect to $(1, 0)$.
4. Scalar versus vector projection of $(2, 1)$ onto $(0, 1)$.
5. Reflect $d = (1, -1, 0)$ off a surface with unit normal $n = (0, 1, 0)$.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Scalar $3$; vector $(3, 0)$.
2. Scalar $7/\sqrt 2 \approx 4.95$; vector $(3.5, 3.5)$.
3. $(3, 4) - (3, 0) = (0, 4)$.
4. Scalar $1$ (the y component); vector $(0, 1)$.
5. $d \cdot n = -1$; $r = d - 2(-1)\,n = (1, -1, 0) + (0, 2, 0) = (1, 1, 0)$.

</details>

## Python

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3., 4.])
b = np.array([1., 1.])
bh = b / np.linalg.norm(b)          # unit direction

scalar = a @ bh                      # length of the shadow
proj = scalar * bh                   # the shadow as a vector
perp = a - proj                      # the leftover
print(scalar)                        # 4.949747...  (= 7 / sqrt 2)
print(proj)                          # [3.5 3.5]
print(perp)                          # [-0.5  0.5]
print(proj @ perp)                   # 0.0 (up to rounding): perpendicular
print((a @ b) / (b @ b) * b)         # [3.5 3.5]   same, without normalizing b

# coordinates are shadows on the axes
e1, e2 = np.array([1., 0.]), np.array([0., 1.])
print(a @ e1, a @ e2)                # 3.0 4.0

# slide along the floor, and reflect
v = np.array([2., -3., 1.]); n = np.array([0., 1., 0.])
print(v - (v @ n) * n)               # [2. 0. 1.]
d = np.array([1., -1., 0.])
print(d - 2 * (d @ n) * n)           # [1. 1. 0.]
```

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: many shadows at once</summary>

Projecting one vector onto $k$ unit directions is $k$ dot products with the same input. Stack the $k$ directions as rows and the whole thing is one matrix–vector product (a GEMV in BLAS). That is the first honest glimpse of what a matrix is in this course: a bundle of dot products applied together. Part 2 makes it precise.

</details>
