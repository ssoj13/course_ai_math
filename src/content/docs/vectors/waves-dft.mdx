**Built on:** [The dot product](lesson:dot-product) · [Projection](lesson:projection) · [Basis](lesson:basis) · [The unit circle](lesson:unit-circle)

An audio clip is a long list of numbers. So is one row of pixels in a texture, and so is an animation curve baked to
one value per frame. This lesson takes one idea seriously: **a list of numbers is a vector**. Then everything we
built for arrows (length, the dot product, projection, bases) works on sounds and curves too.

We will ask one question of a signal: *how much of a given wave is in it?* The answer is a single dot product. Ask it
for every possible wave and you get the famous **discrete Fourier transform** (DFT), the thing behind every audio
equalizer, JPEG, FFT ocean in a VFX shot and convolution bloom in a game engine. At the end the same numbers turn into
a toy: sticks spinning on the ends of other sticks, which can redraw any drawing you give them.

Nothing here needs complex numbers. Every step is "multiply matching numbers and add them up".

## 1. A sampled signal is a vector

Take a wave and read its height at 16 evenly spaced moments: $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 15$. You get 16 numbers,
$x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{15}$. That list is the **sampled signal** $x$.

Here is the one we start with (rounded to two decimals):

$$
\begin{aligned} x = (&1.20,\ 1.40,\ -0.42,\ -1.08, \\ &-0.60,\ -1.08,\ -0.42,\ 1.40, \\ &1.20,\ 0.29,\ 0.42,\ -0.62, \\ &-1.80,\ -0.62,\ 0.42,\ 0.29) \end{aligned}
$$

In [lesson 1.1](lesson:vector) a vector with 3 components was an arrow in 3D. This one has 16 components, so it is an
arrow in 16-dimensional space. Nobody can draw 16 axes, and we do not need to: the arithmetic does not care how many
components there are. Length is still the root of the sum of squares, and the dot product is still "multiply matching
components and add".

What we *can* draw is the list itself: one dot per sample, its height the value. That is the top lane of the scene
below. Think of a curve in the Graph Editor baked to 16 keys: the keys are the vector.

## 2. Rulers: one wave per frequency

To measure "how much of a wave" we need the waves to measure with. Go back to [the unit circle](lesson:unit-circle):
a point going around the circle has $\cos$ as its shadow on the x axis. Let the point go around exactly $k$ times
while $n$ runs from 0 to 16, and read its shadow at every sample:

$$
c_k[n] = \cos\!\left(2\pi \cdot k \cdot n / 16\right)
$$

That list of 16 numbers is the **cosine ruler** of frequency $k$. In words: $k$ full waves fit in the window.

- $k = 0$: the point never moves, every number is 1. A flat line, the "average level" ruler.
- $k = 1$: one slow wave over the whole window.
- $k = 2$: two waves: $c_2 = (1, 0.71, 0, -0.71, -1, \ldots)$.
- $k = 8$: the fastest wave 16 samples can hold, $(1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots)$: up, down, up, down.

The **sine ruler** $s_k[n] = \sin(2\pi \cdot k \cdot n / 16)$ is the same wave started a quarter turn later.

Why two rulers per frequency? A wave of frequency $k$ can be shifted left or right in time. Every shifted version is
some amount of the cosine ruler plus some amount of the sine ruler, just as every direction in the plane is some
amount of x plus some amount of y. The two rulers of one frequency are its "x and y axes".

## 3. The dot product says how much

Now the question. How much does the signal point along the ruler $r$? Exactly what [lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product)
answers:

$$
x \cdot r = \sum_{n=0}^{15} x_n\, r_n
$$

Multiply sample by sample, add the 16 products. The scene shows it in three lanes: the signal, the ruler, and the 16
products. Each product is one stem (dashed when it is negative); the sum of the stems is the dot product.

Drag a dot of the signal, or pick another ruler with $k$ and the **sin** switch in the N panel (on a phone: the chips
under the scene), or scrub $k$ in the formula.

Read the numbers for our signal:

- $x \cdot c_2 = 9.60$. Big and positive: the signal points strongly along the $k = 2$ cosine ruler.
- $x \cdot s_5 = 4.80$. Also positive: there is a $k = 5$ sine wave in it too.
- $x \cdot c_3 = 0$. Zero: the signal is **perpendicular** to this ruler. There is no $k = 3$ wave in it at all.
- A negative result would mean the wave is in there upside down.

This is the same sentence as `dot(N, L)` in a shader: N·L says how much a normal faces the light; $x \cdot c_k$ says how
much the signal "faces" the wave $k$.

**How many copies?** A dot product also grows with the length of the ruler. To get the number of *copies* of the ruler,
divide by the ruler's squared length, exactly the projection coefficient of [lesson 1.5](lesson:projection). The
number of copies of $r$ in $x$ is

$$
\frac{x \cdot r}{r \cdot r}
$$

Every ruler here has $r \cdot r = 8$, except $c_0$ and $c_8$, which have 16 (all their numbers are $\pm 1$). So:
$9.60 / 8 = 1.2$ copies of $c_2$, and $4.80 / 8 = 0.6$ copies of $s_5$. That is exactly how the signal was made:
$x = 1.2\, c_2 + 0.6\, s_5$.

One honest oddity: $s_0$ and $s_8$ are all zeros (the sine is 0 at every sample: $\sin 0 = 0$ and $\sin(\pi n) = 0$).
A ruler of zeros measures nothing, so those two drop out.

**Misconception to drop:** "the DFT is some special wave magic." It is 16 numbers multiplied by 16 numbers and added up,
once per ruler. You already know the operation.

## 4. The rulers are perpendicular to each other

Here is the fact that makes everything clean. Take any two *different* rulers and dot them: you always get 0.

$$
\begin{aligned} &c_j \cdot c_k = 0, \quad s_j \cdot s_k = 0 \qquad (j \ne k) \\ &c_j \cdot s_k = 0 \end{aligned}
$$

The grid under the spectrum below shows all 256 dot products of the 16 rulers ($c_0, \ldots, c_8$ and
$s_1, \ldots, s_7$): zeros everywhere off the diagonal, 8 or 16 on it.

Why care? [Lesson 1.6](lesson:basis) said: with perpendicular rulers, the coordinates of a vector are just its shadows
(dot products divided by squared lengths). No equations to solve. And 16 perpendicular non-zero rulers in a
16-dimensional space are a **basis**: they can describe every possible signal.

Pythagoras comes along for free. The squared length of the signal equals the sum of the squared shadows:

$$
\sum_n x_n^2 = \sum_k \frac{(x \cdot r_k)^2}{r_k \cdot r_k}
$$

Check with our signal: the left side is $14.40$. On the right only two rulers are non-zero:
$9.6^2 / 8 + 4.8^2 / 8 = 11.52 + 2.88 = 14.40$. Same number. This is called **Parseval's theorem**; here it is
simply Pythagoras in the ruler basis.

## 5. The DFT is the whole list

Do the dot product for every frequency $k = 0, 1, \ldots, 8$, with both rulers. That list is the **DFT** of the signal; we write its entry for frequency $k$ as $X[k]$:

$$
X[k] = \big(x \cdot c_k,\; x \cdot s_k\big), \qquad k = 0, \ldots, 8
$$

Drawn as bars, it is the **spectrum**: for each $k$ a left bar (copies of the cosine ruler) and a right bar (copies of
the sine ruler). Negative bars hang down and are hatched. Click or tap a bar to select its $k$ in every scene.

Count the numbers: 9 frequencies times 2 rulers is 18, minus the two all-zero sine rulers, is 16. Sixteen numbers in,
sixteen numbers out. The DFT throws nothing away: it is the same arrow described in another basis (a **change of
basis**, lesson 1.6).

In NumPy this is `np.fft.rfft(x)`. It packs each pair into one complex number, $x \cdot c_k - i\,(x \cdot s_k)$; the
complex form and the reason for the minus sign wait for lesson A4. The values are exactly these dot products, and the
Python cell below checks it.

## 6. Going back: add the scaled rulers

If the DFT says "1.2 copies of $c_2$ and 0.6 copies of $s_5$", then rebuilding the signal is just doing that: take 1.2
of one ruler, 0.6 of the other, add them sample by sample. That is a [linear combination](lesson:vector-arithmetic),
and it is the **inverse DFT**:

$$
x = \sum_k \left( \frac{x \cdot c_k}{c_k \cdot c_k}\, c_k + \frac{x \cdot s_k}{s_k \cdot s_k}\, s_k \right)
$$

The scene adds the frequencies back one at a time: $K$ says how many (frequencies $0$ to $K - 1$). The solid dots are
the signal, the small dots and the curve the rebuild, the dashed lines what is still missing.

Load the **step** signal (eight $+1$, then eight $-1$) and raise $K$. The sum of squared misses goes
$16 \to 16 \to 2.86 \to 2.86 \to 1.24 \to 1.24 \to 0.52 \to 0.52 \to 0 \to 0$. It never grows (every ruler adds
something perpendicular, so it can only take error away), and with all 9 frequencies it is exactly 0. A sharp step
needs the fast rulers: that is why a hard edge in a texture or an image needs high frequencies.

## 7. Two honest surprises: aliasing and leakage

**Aliasing.** Load **too fast (k = 14)**. The dashed curve is a wave making 14 turns in the window, but look at the 16
dots: they are exactly the dots of the $k = 2$ ruler (times 1.5). Why: one sample later a 14-turn wave has moved
$14/16$ of a turn forward, which lands in the same place as moving $2/16$ of a turn backward. The cosine cannot see
the difference:

$$
\begin{aligned} \tfrac{14}{16} &= 1 - \tfrac{2}{16} \\ \cos\!\left(2\pi \cdot 14 n / 16\right) &= \cos\!\left(2\pi \cdot 2 n / 16\right) \end{aligned}
$$

No list of 16 samples can tell those two waves apart. You have seen this: wagon wheels spinning backwards on film,
moiré on a fine fabric texture, a checkerboard shimmering in the distance without mipmaps. The cure is always the same:
remove frequencies above $N/2$ **before** sampling (blur, prefilter, mip). Frequency 8 here, half the sample count, is
the highest that survives; it is called the **Nyquist** frequency.

**Leakage.** Load **between rulers (k = 2.5)**. This wave makes two and a half turns in the window, and no ruler does.
So no single bar takes it all: the spectrum shows something at every $k$, largest around 2 and 3. The DFT is not wrong
(rebuild it with all $K$ and you get the signal back exactly); it simply describes the signal with the rulers it has.
Picture it like a tiling texture whose pattern does not fit the tile: the DFT treats the window as if it repeated
forever, end glued to start, and 2.5 turns leave a jump at the glue line. Real tools soften that jump by fading the
window's edges first ("windowing").

## 8. The FFT: the same numbers, fast

Computed as written, the DFT of $N$ samples is about $N$ dot products of length $N$: $N^2$ multiplications. For
$N = 16$ that is 256, nothing. For one second of audio, $N = 48000$, it is over two billion.

The **fast Fourier transform** (FFT) gets *exactly the same numbers* with about $N \log_2 N$ multiplications: for
$N = 1024$, about 10 thousand instead of a million. The trick is reuse: the rulers of a big DFT are built from the
rulers of two half-size DFTs, so the work splits in halves again and again. "FFT" is not a different transform; it is
a faster way to do these dot products. Lesson A4 takes it apart.

## 9. Epicycles: sticks on sticks on sticks

Now the toy. Count on your fingers.

**One stick.** Pin one end of a stick and spin it at a steady speed. The free end draws a circle. That is the unit
circle of lesson 0.4, made bigger or smaller by the stick's length.

**Two sticks.** Pin a second stick to the free end of the first, and spin it at a different speed. Like a fairground
ride whose arm turns while the cup on its end turns too, or a Maya hierarchy of two nulls, each rotating at its own
constant speed. The end of the second stick no longer draws a circle: with one stick turning forward and the other
backward at the same speed you get an ellipse; with other speeds, a flower with loops or petals.

**Many sticks.** Each extra stick spins faster and is (usually) shorter, so it adds a finer wiggle on top of what the
others draw. With enough sticks the very last tip can follow **any closed drawing**, a heart, a star, your signature.

Where do the sticks come from? From this lesson. A closed drawing of $M = 64$ points is two lists of numbers: the x
of every point and the y of every point. Two signals. For every frequency $k$, four dot products with the same rulers
(now 64 samples long) give the stick's position at the start:

$$
\begin{aligned} X_k &= \frac{x \cdot c_k + y \cdot s_k}{64} \\ Y_k &= \frac{y \cdot c_k - x \cdot s_k}{64} \end{aligned}
$$

- the stick's **length** is $\sqrt{X_k^2 + Y_k^2}$ (Pythagoras),
- its **starting angle** is the angle of the point $(X_k, Y_k)$ (`atan2`, lesson 0.4),
- its **speed** is $k$ turns per loop; a negative $k$ turns clockwise,
- the $k = 0$ "stick" does not spin at all: it is the average point of the drawing, where the chain is pinned.

Why does it work? A stick spinning $k$ times per loop is a point on a circle, $(\cos, \sin)$: a cosine ruler in x and
a sine ruler in y at the same time. Adding sticks end to end is adding rulers, which is exactly the rebuild of section 6,
done for x and y together.

The heart, for example: pinned at $(0, 0.40)$; the longest stick has length $2.125$, turns once per loop clockwise
($k = -1$) and starts at $90^\circ$; the next ones are $0.51$ ($k = -3$) and $0.425$ ($k = \pm 2$). The error, measured
as the average squared miss in percent of the miss with no stick at all, falls as sticks are added:

| sticks | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| error, % | 12.95 | 7.94 | 4.46 | 0.97 | 0.42 | 0.00 |

Press **Play**, change the number of sticks, pick another drawing, or press **Draw your own** and draw a closed shape
with the mouse or a finger. **Step** moves the pen to the next of the 64 drawn points, where it lands exactly once
every stick is in.

> **Say it simpler.** A sampled wave is an arrow with 16 components. Each frequency gives two rulers (cos and sin).
> The dot product with a ruler says how much of that wave is in the signal; the list of all those dot products is the
> DFT. The rulers are perpendicular, so adding them back, scaled, rebuilds the signal exactly. Spinning sticks are the
> same rulers, drawing in x and y at once.

## By hand

Work these on paper before opening the answers.

1. Write the cosine ruler $c_4$ for $N = 16$ (hint: four turns in 16 samples is a quarter turn per sample).
2. $x = (1, 1, 1, 1)$ with $N = 4$. Compute $x \cdot c_0$ and $x \cdot c_1$, where $c_1 = (1, 0, -1, 0)$.
3. $x = 3\, c_2 - 2\, s_1$ with $N = 16$. How many copies of $c_2$ does the dot product report, and what is $x \cdot s_1$?
4. With $N = 16$, which ruler has the same samples as a cosine making 15 turns?
5. One stick of length 2 starting at angle 0, turning once per loop. Where is its tip at a quarter of the loop?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $c_4 = (1, 0, -1, 0, 1, 0, -1, 0, \ldots)$: the four numbers 1, 0, −1, 0 repeat four times.
2. $x \cdot c_0 = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4$; $x \cdot c_1 = 1 + 0 - 1 + 0 = 0$: a constant signal has no wave in it.
3. $x \cdot c_2 = 3 \cdot (c_2 \cdot c_2) = 3 \cdot 8 = 24$, and $24 / 8 = 3$ copies. $x \cdot s_1 = -2 \cdot 8 = -16$ (every other ruler contributes 0, they are perpendicular).
4. $c_1$: 15 turns forward per window look like 1 turn backward, and the cosine does not see the direction.
5. A quarter turn counter-clockwise from angle 0: the tip is at $(0, 2)$ relative to the pin.

</details>

## In NumPy

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

N = 16
n = np.arange(N)
x = 1.2 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * 2 * n / N) + 0.6 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 5 * n / N)   # "two waves"

def cos_ruler(k):
    return np.cos(2 * np.pi * k * n / N)

def sin_ruler(k):
    return np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * n / N)

print(round(x @ cos_ruler(2), 6))            # 9.6 -> 9.6 / 8 = 1.2 copies
print(round(x @ sin_ruler(5), 6))            # 4.8 -> 4.8 / 8 = 0.6 copies
print(np.isclose(x @ cos_ruler(3), 0))       # True: perpendicular, no k = 3 in x

# The DFT is the list of these dot products; NumPy packs each pair as x.c - i x.s
dots = np.array([x @ cos_ruler(k) - 1j * (x @ sin_ruler(k)) for k in range(N // 2 + 1)])
print(np.allclose(dots, np.fft.rfft(x)))     # True

# 16 rulers: cos 0..8 and sin 1..7. Every pair is perpendicular.
R = np.array([cos_ruler(k) for k in range(9)] + [sin_ruler(k) for k in range(1, 8)])
G = R @ R.T
print(np.allclose(G, np.diag(np.diag(G))))   # True: 0 off the diagonal
print(np.round(np.diag(G)).astype(int))      # [16  8  8  8  8  8  8  8 16  8  8  8  8  8  8  8]

coef = (R @ x) / np.diag(G)                  # copies of each ruler (projection, lesson 1.5)
print(np.allclose(coef @ R, x))              # True: adding the scaled rulers back gives x
print(round(x @ x, 6), round(np.sum((R @ x) ** 2 / np.diag(G)), 6))   # 14.4 14.4  (Parseval)

# Aliasing: 14 turns sampled 16 times look exactly like 2 turns
print(np.allclose(np.cos(2 * np.pi * 14 * n / N), cos_ruler(2)))       # True

# Epicycles of a figure eight drawn with M = 64 points
M = 64
m = np.arange(M)
X, Y = 3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * m / M), 1.6 * np.sin(4 * np.pi * m / M)

def stick(k):
    c, s = np.cos(2 * np.pi * k * m / M), np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * m / M)
    sx, sy = (X @ c + Y @ s) / M, (Y @ c - X @ s) / M   # four dot products
    return np.hypot(sx, sy), np.degrees(np.arctan2(sy, sx))

for k in (1, -1, 2):
    r, a = stick(k)
    print(k, round(r, 3), round(a))          # 1 1.5 -90 / -1 1.5 90 / 2 0.8 0
Z = np.fft.fft(X + 1j * Y) / M               # the same numbers, by FFT (lesson A4)
print(np.allclose(np.abs(Z[[1, -1, 2]]), [stick(k)[0] for k in (1, -1, 2)]))   # True
```

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: N² dot products, N log N butterflies, and rounding</summary>

A direct DFT of $N$ samples is $N$ dot products of length $N$: $N^2$ multiply-adds, each one an FMA of
[lesson 1.4](lesson:dot-product). The FFT reorganizes the same sums: a size-$N$ DFT is two size-$N/2$ DFTs (even and
odd samples) combined with $N/2$ small "butterflies", recursively, for about $N \log_2 N$ operations. For
$N = 2^{20}$ (a million samples) that is roughly 20 million instead of a trillion. Libraries such as FFTW, cuFFT and
NumPy's pocketfft pick the split per size and per CPU or GPU.

The numbers are not bit-identical between the two routes. Floating-point addition is not associative, so summing the
same products in another order changes the last bits ([summation order](lesson:dot-product)); in the tests of this
lesson the dot-product DFT and an FFT agree to about $10^{-14}$. In VFX you meet the FFT in Tessendorf ocean
displacement (an inverse FFT of a wave spectrum per frame), in convolution bloom and large blurs (multiply spectra
instead of sliding a huge kernel), and in every audio tool.

</details>

## What this opens

Every dot product of this lesson used the same 16 rulers. Stack them as the rows of a table, and one operation could
compute all the dot products at once: can a **matrix** do that? Part 2 answers yes; this table is the DFT matrix.
Lesson A4 then brings the complex numbers that pack each cosine and sine pair into one spinning arrow, takes the FFT
apart, and shows the same spinning rulers inside RoPE, the position encoding of modern transformers.
