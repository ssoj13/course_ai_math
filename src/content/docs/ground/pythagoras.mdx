## How far apart are two points?

Two points on the Maya grid plane (ignore height for now): $P = (1, 2)$ and $Q = (4, 6)$. You call `distance(P, Q)` or
`length(Q - P)` and get a number. This lesson is about what that function computes, why it has squares
inside, and why the same formula keeps working when the points have 3 coordinates, or 4, or 4096.

## Walk east, then north

Start at $P = (1, 2)$. To reach $Q = (4, 6)$ you can walk along the grid: first east along x, then north
along y.

How far east? From $x = 1$ to $x = 4$: that is $4 - 1 = 3$. Call it $dx = 3$.

How far north? From $y = 2$ to $y = 6$: that is $6 - 2 = 4$. Call it $dy = 4$.

The grid walk is $3 + 4 = 7$ units long. That is the **Manhattan distance**: the length of a trip on city
streets. But a straight line from $P$ to $Q$ is shorter than the grid walk, the way a flight is shorter than
the drive. The question is: how much shorter?

Drag $P$ and $Q$ (or type them in the panel). The east leg is blue, the north leg pink, the straight line teal.
On each of the three sides a square is drawn, pointing outward, with its area written inside.

Look at the picture. The east leg, the north leg and the straight line form a triangle with a square corner
where the legs meet. The straight line is the longest side, the **hypotenuse**. Our question is: given the
two legs, how long is the hypotenuse?

## Pythagoras: the squares on the legs fill the square on the hypotenuse

Build a square on each side of the triangle, pointing outward: the picture above already did. Three squares. Their
areas are $dx^2$, $dy^2$ and $c^2$, where $c$ is the length of the hypotenuse (in the scene it is called $d$, for
distance). Hover a square in the scene or a term in the formula: both light up together.

Pythagoras' theorem says the two small areas add up to the big one:

$$
c^2 = dx^2 + dy^2
$$

With our numbers: $dx^2 = 3^2 = 9$ and $dy^2 = 4^2 = 16$. So $c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, and $c = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
The straight line is 5 units long. The grid walk was 7. Flying saves two units.

Read the formula as areas, not as symbols: a 9-tile square plus a 16-tile square has exactly as many tiles as
a 25-tile square. That is the whole theorem. The length comes out at the end with a root, because the root
turns an area back into a side.

### Why it is true

Take the right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$. Make four copies. Arrange them inside a big
square of side $a + b$, each copy with its right angle sitting in one corner of the big square. The four hypotenuses now
fence off a tilted square in the middle, of side $c$.

Step through the proof below with Next and Back, or drag the step slider. The pieces only slide: none of them turns
or changes shape, so no area is created or lost. Change $a$ and $b$ in the panel and the whole proof is redrawn
for the new triangle.

Count the big square's area two ways.

As one square: $(a + b)^2$.

As pieces: four triangles plus the tilted square. Each triangle has area $\tfrac{1}{2} a b$ (half a rectangle
$a$ by $b$), so the four together are $2ab$. Plus the middle square, $c^2$. Total: $2ab + c^2$.

Now slide the four triangles into a different arrangement inside the same big square: two of them make an
$a \times b$ rectangle in one corner, the other two make a $b \times a$ rectangle in the opposite corner. What is left
uncovered is now two squares, one of side $a$ and one of side $b$. Same four triangles, same big square, so the
uncovered area is the same: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

With $a = 3$, $b = 4$: the big square has side 7 and area 49. Four triangles take $4 \cdot 6 = 24$. What is
left is $49 - 24 = 25$. Arranged one way it is the square on the hypotenuse, $c^2 = 25$; arranged the other
way it is $9 + 16$. No algebra was needed, only moving paper.

## The distance formula

Put it together. For two points $P = (x_1, y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2, y_2)$:

$$
d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}
$$

Read it in steps: subtract the coordinates to get the legs, square each leg, add the squares, take the root.
Line by line for $P = (1, 2)$, $Q = (4, 6)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 - x_1 &= 4 - 1 = 3 \\
y_2 - y_1 &= 6 - 2 = 4 \\
3^2 + 4^2 &= 9 + 16 = 25 \\
d &= \sqrt{25} = 5
\end{aligned}
$$

### The sign of a leg does not matter

What if you subtract the other way round, $Q$ from $P$? Then $dx = 1 - 4 = -3$ and $dy = 2 - 6 = -4$.
Squares forget the sign: $(-3)^2 = 9$ and $(-4)^2 = 16$, the same 25, the same $d = 5$. The distance from $P$
to $Q$ equals the distance from $Q$ to $P$, and the formula knows it without being told. This is the first
payoff of "squares forget the sign" from the previous lesson.

### A common wrong shortcut

People in a hurry write $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = a + b$. It is not true. For $a = 3$, $b = 4$: the left side is
$\sqrt{25} = 5$, the right side is $7$. The root of a sum is not the sum of the roots. The grid walk (7) and the
flight (5) are different numbers, and the formula is the one that tells them apart.

## 3D: the same thing twice

Now the points have three coordinates. Maya and Houdini are y-up: x and z run across the floor, y is the height.
Take $P = (0, 0, 0)$ and $Q = (1, 2, 2)$. The legs are $dx = 1 - 0 = 1$, $dy = 2 - 0 = 2$ (up) and
$dz = 2 - 0 = 2$.

Picture a room: the box with opposite corners $P$ and $Q$. Walk across the floor: that is the 2D problem, with
legs $dx$ and $dz$. The floor diagonal $f$ satisfies $f^2 = dx^2 + dz^2 = 1 + 4 = 5$. Now go up by $dy$. The floor
diagonal and the vertical leg form a *new* right triangle, standing upright, and its hypotenuse is the straight line
from $P$ to $Q$. Pythagoras again:

$$
d^2 = f^2 + dy^2 = (dx^2 + dz^2) + dy^2
$$

So $d^2 = 5 + 4 = 9$ and $d = 3$. Two uses of Pythagoras, one after the other, give a formula with three
squares under the root:

$$
d = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2}
$$

Step through it in 3D. Orbit the view by dragging empty space: the floor triangle lies flat, the second triangle
stands upright, and both right angles stay square from every side. Drag $P$ and $Q$ (with a point focused, PageUp
and PageDown move it along z).

This is `length(Q - P)` in a shader and `distance()` in VEX. Nothing new happened in 3D except that one more
square joined the sum. The order of the legs does not matter either: going up first and across the floor second
gives the same three squares.

## n dimensions: nothing changes except the count

An embedding in a language model has 4096 coordinates. A gray-scale image of 28 by 28 pixels is a point
with 784 coordinates. How far apart are two of those?

The same formula, with more terms in the sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d^2 &= (q_1 - p_1)^2 + (q_2 - p_2)^2 \\
&\quad + \cdots + (q_n - p_n)^2 \\
d &= \sqrt{d^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

A 4D example you can do by hand: $P = (0, 0, 0, 0)$ and $Q = (1, 0, 2, 2)$. The legs are $1, 0, 2, 2$.
Squares: $1, 0, 4, 4$. Sum: $9$. Root: $d = 3$.

No picture this time, only the numbers, because no picture is needed. Below, $P$ and $Q$ are lists of numbers:
scrub any of them (drag sideways, or focus one and use the arrow keys), and change the number of dimensions $n$
with the buttons. Each row of the table is one dimension: the difference and its square. The sum at the bottom is
$d^2$, its root is $d$. Going from 4 to 5 dimensions adds one row and one term, and nothing else changes.

<PythagorasND locale={locale} client:load />

Here is the misconception to drop: "in four or more dimensions I cannot picture the triangle, so the
formula must need some geometry I do not have." It needs none. The 3D derivation was "Pythagoras on the
floor, then Pythagoras standing up." The 4D derivation is the same with one more step, and so on. You do not
need to see the room; you need to square, add and root. Numbers do not care how many of them there are.

## Compare squares, skip the root

You already know the habit from the previous lesson: CG code writes `dist2 < r * r` instead of
`length(d) < r`. Now you can see why it is safe. Both $d$ and $r$ are lengths, never negative, and for numbers
that are not negative the bigger number always has the bigger square. So $d < r$ and $d^2 < r^2$ ask the same
question. The squared version needs no root: in 3D it is three multiplies and two adds.

Drag $X$ around the circle and change $r$. The first line of the formula compares the squares, the second compares
the lengths: they always give the same answer, inside, on the circle or outside.

The same trick sorts neighbours: sorting by $d^2$ gives the same order as sorting by $d$. It breaks only when a sign
can sneak in: $-3 < 2$, but $(-3)^2 = 9 > 4 = 2^2$. Compare squares only of numbers that are never negative, like
lengths.

> **Say it simpler.** Walk east, then north. The straight path is shorter, and Pythagoras says exactly how much:
> square each leg, add, take the root. Three legs, four legs, four thousand legs: same recipe.

## By hand

1. Distance from $(0, 0)$ to $(3, 4)$.
2. Distance from $(1, 2)$ to $(4, 6)$.
3. Distance from $(1, 1, 1)$ to $(2, 3, 3)$.
4. Which is farther from the origin, $(5, 0)$ or $(3, 4)$?
5. Manhattan and straight-line distance from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Legs 3 and 4. $9 + 16 = 25$, $\sqrt{25} = 5$.
2. Legs $4 - 1 = 3$ and $6 - 2 = 4$. Same squares, same answer: $5$.
3. Legs $1, 2, 2$. Squares $1, 4, 4$. Sum $9$. $\sqrt{9} = 3$.
4. $(5, 0)$: $\sqrt{25 + 0} = 5$. $(3, 4)$: $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$. A tie. Many different points sit at the same distance; the next lesson is about all the points at distance 1.
5. Manhattan: $1 + 1 = 2$. Straight: $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$. The diagonal of a unit square.

</details>

## In NumPy

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
q = np.array([4.0, 6.0])

legs = q - p                          # [3. 4.]
print(legs, legs**2)                  # [3. 4.] [ 9. 16.]
d_manual = np.sqrt((legs**2).sum())   # square, add, root
d_numpy = np.linalg.norm(q - p)       # the library's name for the same thing
print(d_manual, d_numpy)              # 5.0 5.0

print(np.abs(legs).sum())             # 7.0 -> Manhattan distance, the grid walk

# 3D and 4D: the same two lines, nothing else changes
p3, q3 = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]), np.array([2.0, 3.0, 3.0])
print(np.linalg.norm(q3 - p3))        # 3.0
p4, q4 = np.zeros(4), np.array([1.0, 0.0, 2.0, 2.0])
print(np.linalg.norm(q4 - p4))        # 3.0
```

</PyCell>

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: hypot, overflow and squared comparisons</summary>

`np.hypot(a, b)` and C's `std::hypot` compute $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ without ever forming $a^2$ in memory.
Why bother? Because squaring can overflow when the legs do not. In fp64 the largest number is about
$1.8 \times 10^{308}$, so a leg of $10^{200}$ has a square of $10^{400}$: infinity. In fp32 the limit is about
$3.4 \times 10^{38}$, so legs above about $1.8 \times 10^{19}$ already break. The same happens at the small end: a
leg of $10^{-200}$ squares to zero (underflow), and the distance between two nearby points comes out as 0.
`hypot` rescales by the larger leg first, computes $\sqrt{1 + (b/a)^2}$ and scales back.
The plain recipe, and `np.linalg.norm` too, square first and lose the answer:

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

legs = np.array([1e200, 1e200])
print(np.sqrt((legs**2).sum()))   # inf: each square overflowed
print(np.linalg.norm(legs))       # inf: norm squares as well
print(np.hypot(legs[0], legs[1])) # 1.414213562373095e+200
```

</PyCell>

For most CG code the legs are world units and nothing overflows. The habit that does matter every day is the one
from the last lesson: when you only need to *compare* distances, compare the squared sums and skip the root.
Sorting neighbours by `dist2` gives the same order as sorting by `dist`.

The half-precision version of this story (a sum of 100 squares overflowing fp16 at 65504) waits for the lesson on
[Length, Σ notation and normalization](lesson:length).

</details>

## What this opens

Distance says *how far*, not *which way*. $(5, 0)$ and $(3, 4)$ are both 5 units from the origin, and the distance
formula cannot tell them apart. To describe a direction with numbers we need the unit circle:
[Angles, radians and the unit circle](lesson:unit-circle).
