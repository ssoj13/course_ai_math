## Two sticks and a gap

Two sticks, one of length $a = 3$ and one of length $b = 4$, joined at one end. Open them to an angle $\theta$.
How long is the gap $c$ between their free ends?

You have rigged this. The upper arm and the forearm are the sticks, the elbow is the joint, and the gap is the
distance from shoulder to wrist. Every two-bone IK solver in Maya, Houdini or a game engine answers this
question many times per frame, and the formula it uses is the one on this page. Most people who have used
that solver have never seen the formula's name.

## Start with what already works

At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the sticks make a square corner, and the last two lessons answer the question:
$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, so $c = 5$.

Now close the sticks a little, to $\theta = 60^\circ$. Measured with a ruler (or in the scene below), the gap is
$c \approx 3.61$, so $c^2 \approx 13$.
Pythagoras promised $25$. Twelve units of area are missing. Where did they go?

Build the three squares on the sides, as in the Pythagoras lesson: $a^2 = 9$ on side $a$, $b^2 = 16$ on side
$b$, $c^2 = 13$ on side $c$. The two small ones are too big by 12 in total. Here is where the 12 is hiding.

**Step 1: the shadow.** Shine a light straight down onto side $a$. Side $b$ casts a shadow on it. How long is
that shadow? The unit circle lesson says: a stick of length 1 at angle $\theta$ has an x shadow of $\cos\theta$.
A stick of length $b$ has a shadow $b$ times as long. So the shadow of $b$ on $a$ is

$$
b\cos\theta = 4 \cdot 0.5 = 2
$$

**Step 2: cut a strip.** Off the square on $a$, cut a strip as wide as that shadow. The strip is $a$ tall and
$b\cos\theta$ wide, so its area is

$$
a \cdot (b\cos\theta) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6
$$

**Step 3: the other strip.** Now light side $b$ instead. Side $a$ casts a shadow of length $a\cos\theta = 3 \cdot 0.5 = 1.5$
on it. Cut a strip of that width off the square on $b$: it is $b$ tall and $a\cos\theta$ wide, area

$$
b \cdot (a\cos\theta) = 4 \cdot 1.5 = 6
$$

The same number. Two rectangles with different shapes, 3 by 2 and 4 by 1.5, and the same area, because
both are $a \cdot b \cdot \cos\theta$ with the factors in a different order. They have the same *shape* only when
$a = b$: then both are $a$ by $a\cos\theta$. (The flat angles, $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, are the one exception:
there the shadow of each stick is its full length, with a minus sign at $180^\circ$, so the strips are $a$ by $b$ and
$b$ by $a$, the same rectangle turned on its side whatever $a$ and $b$ are.)

**Step 4: count.** Two strips of 6 make 12. That is exactly what was missing: $25 - 12 = 13 = c^2$. What is
left of the two squares after the strips are cut fits exactly into the square on $c$. With numbers: the square
on $a$ minus its strip leaves $9 - 6 = 3$, the square on $b$ minus its strip leaves $16 - 6 = 10$, and
$3 + 10 = 13 = c^2$. The square on $c$ splits into two rectangles of exactly those areas along the line of the
height dropped from $C$. In the scene you can watch the leftovers do it: each leftover rectangle leans into a
parallelogram (same base, same height, same area), turns a quarter turn around the far end of its side ($B$ for
the leftover of $a$, $A$ for the leftover of $b$), and leans back upright inside $c^2$. Leaning keeps the base and
the height, turning keeps everything, so each leftover arrives with its area intact: $3$ and $10$ go in, $3 + 10 = 13$
comes out. The same bookkeeping works for a wide corner, where a strip counts as *negative* area and a leftover is
then bigger than its square; the section *Correction term* below walks through $\theta = 120^\circ$ with numbers.

So for a corner that is not square, the rule is:

$$
c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2\,ab\cos\theta
$$

This is the **law of cosines**: Pythagoras, minus two strips. Each strip is a side times the shadow of the other
side on it.

One more case, because the scene will show it as soon as you drag the vertices. Take a short side and a long one at
a sharp angle: $a = 1$, $b = 4$, $\theta = 30^\circ$. The shadow of $b$ on $a$ is $b\cos\theta = 4 \cdot 0.866 \approx 3.464$,
longer than side $a = 1$ itself. The strip is wider than the square it is cut from, so what is left,
$DB = a - b\cos\theta \approx -2.464$, is *negative*: the scene draws that leftover hatched, as a debt. Nothing has
gone wrong. The angle $\theta$ at $C$ is sharp, but the angle at $B$ is then wide, and the two leftovers still add
up to $c^2$ with their signs. The other leftover is $b \cdot EA$, where $EA = b - a\cos\theta = 4 - 0.866$ is what
the shadow of $a$ leaves of side $b$: $1 \cdot (-2.464) + 4 \cdot 3.134 \approx -2.464 + 12.536 = 10.072 \approx c^2$.
(A wide $\theta$ is the other direction: the shadow itself is negative and $DB$ is longer than $a$.)

## The formula, line by line

The symbols: $a$ and $b$ are the two sides that meet at the corner, $\theta$ is the angle *between them*, and
$c$ is the side *opposite* $\theta$, the gap. Get this pairing right and the rest is arithmetic. With
$a = 3$, $b = 4$, $\theta = 60^\circ$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos 60^\circ &= 0.5 \\
2\,ab\cos\theta &= 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 0.5 = 12 \\
c^2 &= 9 + 16 - 12 = 13 \\
c &= \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61
\end{aligned}
$$

A misconception to name: "the law of cosines needs a right angle somewhere." It needs none. It works for every
triangle. When the corner *is* square, $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, the correction term is $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 0 = 0$,
and the formula *becomes* Pythagoras. Pythagoras is not a different law; it is this law at one angle.

## Correction term

Keep $a = 3$ and $b = 4$ and sweep the angle. The term $-2ab\cos\theta$ is the only thing that changes.

| $\theta$ | $\cos\theta$ | $-2ab\cos\theta$ | $c^2$ | $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0^\circ$ | $1$ | $-24$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $60^\circ$ | $0.5$ | $-12$ | $13$ | $3.61$ |
| $90^\circ$ | $0$ | $0$ | $25$ | $5$ |
| $120^\circ$ | $-0.5$ | $+12$ | $37$ | $6.08$ |
| $180^\circ$ | $-1$ | $+24$ | $49$ | $7$ |

Read the two ends first. At $\theta = 0^\circ$ the sticks lie on top of each other and the gap is the difference
of their lengths: $c = |a - b| = 1$. At $\theta = 180^\circ$ they point opposite ways and the gap is the sum:
$c = a + b = 7$. Everything else is in between, and $c$ can never exceed $a + b$ (the triangle inequality: you
cannot be farther from the shoulder than the two bones laid straight).

Now the sign, which is the part people get backwards.

- **Sharp corner, $\theta$ below $90^\circ$:** $\cos\theta$ is positive, the strips are real, the term
  *subtracts*, and $c$ is *shorter* than $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.
- **Square corner:** the shadow has length zero, the strips vanish, Pythagoras.
- **Wide corner, $\theta$ above $90^\circ$:** $\cos\theta$ is negative, so the shadow points *backwards*,
  past the corner. The strip now has negative area (hatched in the scene), and subtracting a negative strip
  means *adding*. $c$ is *longer* than $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$: at $120^\circ$, $c^2 = 25 + 12 = 37$.

So the correction is not "always a subtraction". The strips, $2ab\cos\theta$, carry the sign of $\cos\theta$; the
term in the formula, $-2ab\cos\theta$, has the opposite sign: it takes area away for a sharp corner and adds area
for a wide one. And $\cos\theta$ is negative for wide corners because, as the unit circle lesson showed, a point
past $90^\circ$ sits on the left half of the circle.

If scissors feel like a trick, here is the formula coming out of symbols. The triangle has corner $C$ where
$a$ and $b$ meet at angle $\theta$; side $a$ runs from $C$ to $B$, side $b$ from $C$ to $A$, and $c$ is $AB$.

Drop a perpendicular from $A$ onto the line of side $a$. Call its foot $D$. Now there *are* right angles,
and three signed quantities from the unit circle (ordinary lengths use their absolute values):

$$
\begin{aligned}
CD &= b\cos\theta \\
AD &= b\sin\theta \\
DB &= a - b\cos\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

$CD$ is the shadow of $b$ on $a$ (the same shadow as above). $AD$ is the height. $DB$ is what is left of side $a$
after the shadow. With our numbers: $CD = 2$, $AD = 4\sin60^\circ = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.464$, $DB = 3 - 2 = 1$.

Triangle $ADB$ has a square corner at $D$, so Pythagoras applies to it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c^2 &= DB^2 + AD^2 \\
&= (a - b\cos\theta)^2 \\
&\quad + (b\sin\theta)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Check with numbers before expanding: $1^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2 = 1 + 12 = 13$. Correct. Now expand the first square,
$(a - b\cos\theta)^2 = a^2 - 2ab\cos\theta + b^2\cos^2\theta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c^2 &= a^2 - 2ab\cos\theta \\
&\quad + b^2\cos^2\theta + b^2\sin^2\theta \\
&= a^2 + b^2\,(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) \\
&\quad - 2ab\cos\theta \\
    &= a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step uses $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, which is nothing but "the unit-circle point is at
distance 1" ($x^2 + y^2 = 1$). The middle term, $-2ab\cos\theta$, fell out of expanding the square; it is the
two strips of the first scene, written as algebra.

If $\theta$ is wider than $90^\circ$, $b\cos\theta$ is negative and $D$ lands *behind* $C$, so $DB$ is longer
than $a$. The derivation does not change a single line; the signs take care of themselves.

## Solving for the angle

The formula has four quantities. Given three sides, you can turn it around and find the angle:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}
$$

Example: an equilateral triangle, $a = b = c = 1$. Then $\cos\theta = (1 + 1 - 1) / 2 = 0.5$, and
$\theta = \arccos(0.5) = 60^\circ$. As it should be: three equal sides, three equal corners, $180^\circ / 3$.

Two small conditions, both natural. The sides $a$ and $b$ sit in the denominator, so neither may be zero. And the
three lengths must be able to form a triangle: each one no longer than the other two together. If they cannot, the
fraction falls outside $[-1, 1]$ and no angle has that cosine; the two-bone solver below runs into exactly this.
The limits are the flat triangles: $\cos\theta = 1$ gives $\theta = 0^\circ$ (the sticks lie together, $c = |a - b|$)
and $\cos\theta = -1$ gives $\theta = 180^\circ$ ($c = a + b$).

## Two-bone IK: the law of cosines in your rig

A shoulder at the origin, an upper arm of length $L_1 = 2$, a forearm of length $L_2 = 1.5$, and a hand target.
The solver must choose the elbow angle $\varphi$ so that the hand reaches the target. Three lengths in, one
angle out: that is the turned-around law of cosines with $a = L_1$, $b = L_2$ and $c = d$, the distance from
shoulder to target.

Put the target at $(2.5, 1.5)$. First the distance, by Pythagoras: $d^2 = 2.5^2 + 1.5^2 = 6.25 + 2.25 = 8.5$,
so $d \approx 2.92$. Then the elbow:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos\varphi &= \frac{L_1^2 + L_2^2 - d^2}{2\,L_1 L_2} \\
&= \frac{4 + 2.25 - 8.5}{6} \\
&= \frac{-2.25}{6} = -0.375
\end{aligned}
$$

$\varphi = \arccos(-0.375) \approx 112^\circ$. A negative cosine, so the elbow is opened wider than a square
corner ($112^\circ$ between the bones, where $180^\circ$ would be a straight arm). That is right: the hand is
farther away than the $\sqrt{4 + 2.25} = 2.5$ that a square elbow would reach.

Move the target to distance $d = 2.5$ exactly, say $(2.5, 0)$: $\cos\varphi = (6.25 - 6.25) / 6 = 0$ and
$\varphi = 90^\circ$. The strips vanish, the elbow is square. That is the Pythagoras case inside the rig.

### Why this solver clamps

Now drag the target out of reach, to $d = 4$:

$$
\cos\varphi = \frac{6.25 - 16}{6} = -1.625
$$

No angle has a cosine of $-1.625$: the cosine of any angle lies between $-1$ and $1$. Ask NumPy anyway and
`np.arccos(-1.625)` answers `nan`, "not a number", with a warning; Python's `math.acos` refuses with a `ValueError`.
A NaN elbow angle would make the bone vanish from the screen. So before calling `arccos`, the solver *clamps* the
cosine: anything below $-1$ becomes $-1$, anything above $1$ becomes $1$. Here $-1.625$ becomes $-1$, and
$\arccos(-1) = 180^\circ$: the arm points straight at the target and stops short of it. That is one reasonable
policy, the simplest; a rig can also stretch the bones or ease into the limit with soft IK.

The other end: a target closer than $|L_1 - L_2| = 0.5$, say $d = 0.3$, gives $\cos\varphi = (6.25 - 0.09) / 6 \approx 1.027$,
clamped to $1$, $\varphi = 0^\circ$, the arm folded flat.

```python
import numpy as np

L1, L2 = 2.0, 1.5
shoulder, hand = np.array([0.0, 0.0]), np.array([2.5, 1.5])
d   = np.linalg.norm(hand - shoulder)
c   = (L1*L1 + L2*L2 - d*d) / (2*L1*L2)
c   = np.clip(c, -1.0, 1.0)      # finite positive bone lengths: out of reach -> 180°, too close -> 0°
phi = np.arccos(c)               # elbow angle, radians
print(round(d, 4), round(c, 4), round(np.degrees(phi), 2))   # 2.9155 -0.375 112.02
```

The solver needs one more angle to place the elbow, the shoulder's offset from the shoulder-to-target line,
and it is the same law with the sides relabelled: $\cos\beta = (L_1^2 + d^2 - L_2^2) / (2 L_1 d)$. The last
bit of information, whether the elbow bends to the left or the right of that line, is not in the formula at
all; that is what a pole vector is for.

One special spot: with the target exactly on the shoulder, $d = 0$, the formula for $\beta$ would divide by zero,
and "the direction to the target" has no meaning. The scene then simply points the arm along $+x$ and sets
$\beta = 0$. And note what $\varphi$ measures: the angle *inside* the elbow, between the two bones, $180^\circ$ for
a straight arm. A rig's rotate channel on the elbow joint usually counts from the straight pose instead, so it
reads $180^\circ - \varphi$: $68^\circ$ for our $112^\circ$.

> **Say it simpler.** Pythagoras, plus a fix-up for a corner that is not square. The fix-up is two strips,
> each a side times the shadow of the other side; it is bigger when the corner is sharper, zero at a square
> corner, and negative (it adds) when the corner is wide.

## By hand

1. $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $\theta = 90^\circ$. Find $c$.
2. $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $\theta = 60^\circ$. Find $c^2$, $c$, and the area of one strip.
3. $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $\theta = 120^\circ$. Find $c$.
4. $a = b = 1$, $c = 1$. Find $\theta$.
5. Bones $L_1 = 2$, $L_2 = 1.5$, target at distance $d = 2.5$. Find the elbow angle $\varphi$.
6. Same bones, target at $d = 0.3$. What does the solver do?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, so $c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$ and $c = 5$. Pythagoras.
2. $c^2 = 9 + 16 - 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 0.5 = 25 - 12 = 13$, $c \approx 3.61$. One strip: $3 \cdot (4 \cdot 0.5) = 6$. Two strips: $12 = 25 - 13$.
3. $\cos 120^\circ = -0.5$, so $c^2 = 25 - 2 \cdot 12 \cdot (-0.5) = 25 + 12 = 37$, $c \approx 6.08$. The strips are $-6$ each and get added.
4. $\cos\theta = (1 + 1 - 1) / (2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1) = 0.5$, so $\theta = 60^\circ$.
5. $\cos\varphi = (4 + 2.25 - 6.25) / 6 = 0$, so $\varphi = 90^\circ$.
6. $\cos\varphi = (6.25 - 0.09) / 6 \approx 1.027$, outside $[-1, 1]$: the target is closer than $|L_1 - L_2| = 0.5$. The solver clamps to $1$ and gives $\varphi = 0^\circ$, the arm folded.

</details>

## In NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

def law_of_cosines(a, b, theta_deg):
    th = np.radians(theta_deg)
    return np.sqrt(a*a + b*b - 2*a*b*np.cos(th))

print(law_of_cosines(3, 4, 90))   # 5.0  (Pythagoras)
print(law_of_cosines(3, 4, 60))   # 3.605...
print(law_of_cosines(3, 4, 120))  # 6.082...

# the two strips: a * (b cos th) and b * (a cos th) are the same number
a, b, th = 3.0, 4.0, np.radians(60)
print(round(a * (b*np.cos(th)), 9), round(b * (a*np.cos(th)), 9))   # 6.0 6.0 (rounded: cos(th) carries a tiny fp error)
print(a*a + b*b - 2*6.0)                        # 13.0 = c^2

# sweep theta: c grows from |a - b| to a + b
for deg in (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180):
    print(f"{deg:4d}°  c = {law_of_cosines(3, 4, deg):.3f}")

# two-bone IK: three lengths in, one angle out, clamp before acos
def elbow_deg(L1, L2, d):
    c = (L1*L1 + L2*L2 - d*d) / (2*L1*L2)
    return np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(c, -1.0, 1.0)))

print(elbow_deg(2, 1.5, 2.5))     # 90.0
print(elbow_deg(2, 1.5, 4.0))     # 180.0 (out of reach, clamped)
print(np.arccos(-1.625))          # nan (and a RuntimeWarning: invalid value encountered in arccos)
```

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: acos, rounding and the clamp</summary>

The clamp is not only for targets that are truly out of reach. Put the hand *exactly* at full stretch,
$d = L_1 + L_2$. On paper $\cos\varphi = -1$ exactly. In fp32 the three squares and the division each round,
and the result can land at $-1.0000001$. That is out of range by one part in ten million, and `acos` still answers
NaN. So clamp the computed cosine before every `acos`, even when the target is in reach on paper: the clamp costs
nothing and turns $-1.0000001$ back into $-1$. It guards this one step only; the bone lengths still have to be
positive numbers, and what the rig does with a target out of reach is a separate decision.

A second, quieter problem lives near the same ends. The slope of `acos` is infinite at $\pm 1$: near a straight or
a folded arm, a tiny rounding error in the cosine becomes a large error in the angle. At $\cos\varphi = -0.99999$
the angle is approximately $179.74^\circ$; at $-0.9999$ it is approximately $179.19^\circ$. A change of $0.00009$ in the cosine moved the elbow
by more than half a degree. A sturdier solver computes the angle with `atan2` from a height and a shadow that it
measures separately, instead of from one cosine. (Computing the height *from* that cosine, as
$\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\varphi}$, does not help: it inherits the same error.) The short formula on this page is teaching
code: it shows where the law of cosines sits inside a rig, not how to make a production solver bulletproof.

</details>

## What this opens

Look at the strip one more time. Its area is the length of side $a$ times the shadow of side $b$ on it. In the
next lessons the sides become arrows, written in bold, $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, with lengths $|\mathbf{a}|$
and $|\mathbf{b}|$. Then the strip is $|\mathbf{a}|\,(|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta)$, and the lesson on
[the dot product](lesson:dot-product) gives that product a name, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ (a new meaning of
the dot: not plain multiplication), and shows how to compute it from coordinates alone, with no angle in sight.
The third side, the gap $c$, is then the arrow $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ from the tip of $\mathbf{b}$ to the tip
of $\mathbf{a}$, and its length is $|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}|$. So the law of cosines reads
$|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}|^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2 + |\mathbf{b}|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$: Pythagoras with
two rectangles cut out.

But first the sticks themselves. They have a length and a direction, and we have been treating them as
"sides". The next lesson, [A vector: an arrow and a list of numbers](lesson:vector), treats them as arrows
everywhere.
