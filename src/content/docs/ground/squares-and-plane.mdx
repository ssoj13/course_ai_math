## Why do we call x² a "square"?

You have typed `x * x` thousands of times. You have also typed `dist2 < r * r` to avoid a square root.
This lesson asks a question most of us skipped at school: why is `x * x` called the *square* of x,
and what does that name buy us?

The answer is a picture, and the picture comes back in every lesson of this course: in Pythagoras, in the length
of a vector, in the variance of a dataset, in the loss of a neural network. Squares are areas. Once you see that,
"why are there squares everywhere in machine learning" stops being a mystery.

## A square is an area

Take a number. Call it $x$. Draw a square whose side has length $x$. The area of that square is $x \cdot x$,
and we write it $x^2$.

$$
x^2 = x \cdot x
$$

A concrete example. Let $x = 3$. The square has side 3. Fill it with unit tiles (tiles of side 1): you get
3 rows of 3 tiles, so 9 tiles. The area is 9, and $3^2 = 3 \cdot 3 = 9$. The number and the picture agree.

One more: $x = 7$ gives $7^2 = 49$. Forty-nine unit tiles, 7 by 7.

{/* scene: 0.G-1 — scrub x in "x² = ?": a literal square of side x grows on the grid, the area readout is the formula value; drag x negative, the square stays and the area stays positive */}

Now a case that surprises people. Let $x = 0.5$. Then $x^2 = 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$. The number $0.25$ is
*smaller* than $0.5$. Squaring does not always make a number bigger: between 0 and 1 it makes it smaller. The picture
explains it: a square with a side of half a tile covers a quarter of a tile.

### Squares forget the sign

What is $(-3)^2$? It is $(-3) \cdot (-3)$. A negative times a negative is a positive, so $(-3)^2 = 9$.
The same 9 as $3^2$.

In the picture: a side of "length $-3$" is a side of length 3 drawn in the other direction. Same square,
same area. The square does not remember which way you drew the side. We will say it like this from now on:
**squares forget the sign**. In a few lessons this is the reason a distance is never negative.

A trap that bites in code. In Python, `-3**2` is `-9`, not `9`, because `**` binds tighter than the minus sign:
Python reads it as `-(3**2)`. The square of $-3$ is `(-3)**2`, with the parentheses. Mathematically $-3^2$ means
the same thing as Python says: minus the square of 3. The notation and the language agree; it is our eyes that
glue the minus to the 3.

## The root undoes the square

Now go backwards. You know the area of a square. What is its side?

The area is 49. The side is the number whose square is 49. That is 7, because $7^2 = 49$. We write this with
the root sign: $\sqrt{49} = 7$.

$$
\sqrt{A} = \text{the side of a square whose area is } A
$$

{/* scene: 0.G-3 — scrub the area, the side appears: the root as the inverse of the square */}

Three things to know about the root.

**Most roots are not round numbers.** $\sqrt{2}$ is about $1.4142$, because $1.4142^2 = 1.99996$, just short of 2.
No decimal hits 2 exactly. That is normal, and a computer is happy with the approximation. Do not expect
roots to be pretty; expect squares to be.

**The root is never negative.** Both $7^2 = 49$ and $(-7)^2 = 49$. So which one is $\sqrt{49}$? By agreement,
the root sign always means the non-negative one: $\sqrt{49} = 7$. A side length is a length.

**Root of a square.** Because squares forget the sign, $\sqrt{a^2}$ is not always $a$. Try $a = -3$:
$a^2 = 9$ and $\sqrt{9} = 3$, which is not $-3$. The correct statement is

$$
\sqrt{a^2} = |a|
$$

where $|a|$ is the absolute value, "$a$ with its sign thrown away". $|{-3}| = 3$ and $|3| = 3$. This looks like a
small point; it is the reason the length of a vector is positive even when every coordinate is negative.

## The plane: two rulers, one point

Now the second idea. A number line is a ruler: each number is a place on it, and each place is a number.
Zero is where you put the start of the ruler.

Lay a second ruler across the first, at a right angle, with their zeros touching. The crossing is the
**origin**. The first ruler is the **x axis**, the second is the **y axis**. Together they make the **plane**.

Any place on the plane is now two readings: how far along the x ruler, and how far along the y ruler.
We write them as a pair in parentheses, x first: $(x, y)$. The pair is called the **coordinates** of the
**point**.

{/* scene: 0.G-2 — number line, then the plane: drag a point, (x, y) updates in the formula and in a 2-row table; the two dashed legs to the axes light up with the matching coordinate */}

An example. The point $(-2, 3)$. Start at the origin. Walk 2 units in the negative x direction (left).
Then walk 3 units in the positive y direction (up). You are at $(-2, 3)$. If you drop a line from the point
straight down to the x axis, it lands on $-2$. If you drop a line sideways to the y axis, it lands on $3$.
The two dashed "legs" from a point to the axes *are* its coordinates, drawn.

You have seen this ten thousand times: the Maya viewport grid with the world origin at its center,
`@P.x` and `@P.y` in a Houdini wrangle, a 2D UV coordinate. The math word for "where is this vertex"
is "what are its coordinates".

One convention to fix now. In this course the y axis points **up**, as in a Maya viewport or a math
textbook. Screen, image and UV coordinates often put y pointing **down** (row 0 at the top). Both are
fine; the formulas in this course never depend on it, but the pictures do, so we say it once here.

### A point and a pair of numbers are one thing

Here is a misconception to defuse before it grows. People think of "the point" as the real thing and
"the coordinates" as a description of it, as if they could disagree. They cannot. Once the two rulers are
fixed, the point $(-2, 3)$ and the pair of numbers $-2, 3$ are the same object seen two ways: one as a dot
on a grid, one as two numbers in memory. Drag the dot and the numbers change; edit the numbers and the dot moves.
There is nothing in between.

This matters because the whole course lives on that bridge. A vector will be an arrow *and* a list of numbers.
A matrix will be a transformation *and* a grid of numbers. Every time, the picture and the numbers are one thing.

### Squares of coordinates

One last step, so the next lesson can begin without a run-up. Take the point $(-2, 3)$ and square each
coordinate:

$$
(-2)^2 = 4, \qquad 3^2 = 9
$$

Two squares: one with area 4 built on the x leg, one with area 9 built on the y leg. Their total area is
$4 + 9 = 13$. Keep that number in mind. [Distance and Pythagoras](lesson:pythagoras) shows what $\sqrt{13}$ measures.

> **Say it simpler.** $x^2$ is the area of a square of side $x$. $\sqrt{A}$ is the side of a square of area
> $A$. A point is two readings on two rulers, and the readings and the dot are the same thing.

## By hand

Work these on paper before opening the answers.

1. $7^2$
2. $(-3)^2$
3. $\sqrt{49}$
4. $\sqrt{2^2 + 2^2}$ (leave it as a root, then estimate)
5. Plot $(-2, 3)$ and read its two legs to the axes.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $7^2 = 49$.
2. $(-3)^2 = 9$. The sign is forgotten.
3. $\sqrt{49} = 7$. Not $-7$: the root sign means the non-negative side.
4. $2^2 + 2^2 = 4 + 4 = 8$, so the answer is $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$ (check: $2.83^2 = 8.01$). Not a round number, and that is fine.
5. Two units left of the origin, three up. The leg down to the x axis lands on $-2$; the leg sideways to the y axis lands on $3$.

## In NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.arange(-3, 4)          # -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3
print(x)
print(x**2)                   # 9 4 1 0 1 4 9  -> squares forget the sign

print(np.sqrt(49.0))          # 7.0
print(np.sqrt(2.0))           # 1.4142135623730951 (its square is 2.0000000000000004)
print(np.sqrt((-3.0)**2))     # 3.0 = |-3|, not -3
print(-3**2, (-3)**2)         # -9 9  -> precedence trap

p = np.array([-2.0, 3.0])     # a point as two numbers
print(p[0]**2 + p[1]**2)      # 13.0 -> lesson 0.3
```

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: why CG code compares squared distances</summary>

`x * x` is one multiply: a single instruction, about as cheap as an add. `sqrt` is also a hardware
instruction on every modern CPU and GPU, but it is a slower one, several times the cost of a multiply (roughly
3 to 10 times on current CPUs), and it cannot be pipelined as freely.

Here is the trick everyone in CG uses without naming it. Squaring keeps order: if $0 \le a \le b$ then
$a^2 \le b^2$, and the other way round. So to ask "is this point closer than $r$?" you never need the root:
compare the squared distance with $r^2$ instead. That is the `dist2 < r * r` you have written in every
spatial query, culling test and particle solver. One multiply instead of one square root, and, apart from points
within a rounding error of the boundary, the answer is the same.

</details>
