## What does pow() actually do?

You have written `pow(c, 2.2)` for gamma, `pow(max(0.0, dot(R, V)), 32.0)` for a highlight, `1.0 / (r * r)` for
a light that fades with distance, and `1.0 / sqrt(d)` in an attention kernel (where $d$ is the length of each
query and key vector, for example 64). Four pieces of code, one operation:
a number raised to a power. The previous lesson showed that $x^2$ is an *area*. This lesson keeps that picture and
asks what the other exponents mean. What is $x^3$? What is $x^0$? What can $x^{-2}$ possibly mean, and why is
$x^{1/2}$ the square root?

The answers all come from a single rule, and the rule comes from counting.

## 1. Whole powers: length, area, volume

A **power** $x^n$ with a whole number $n$ means "multiply $n$ copies of $x$ together". The number on top, $n$,
is the **exponent**; the number below, $x$, is the **base**.

$$
x^1 = x, \qquad x^2 = x \cdot x, \qquad x^3 = x \cdot x \cdot x
$$

Take $x = 2$. Then $2^1 = 2$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^3 = 8$. Take $x = 3$: $3^1 = 3$, $3^2 = 9$, $3^3 = 27$.

Each of the first three powers is a shape you can hold:

- $x^1$ is a **length**: a stick of length $x$.
- $x^2$ is an **area**: a square of side $x$. For $x = 3$ it holds $9$ unit tiles.
- $x^3$ is a **volume**: a cube of edge $x$. For $x = 3$ it holds $27$ unit cubes, three layers of nine.

That is why we say "three *squared*" and "three *cubed*". The words are the shapes.

{/* scene: 0.2-1 — "A square is an area": drag the side x; the square fills with unit tiles counted live (x = 3 → 9 tiles; non-integer x shows partial tiles and the exact area); the N-panel shows x, x² and a √ field that drives it backwards */}

A detail that will matter later. The square with side $2.5$ holds four whole tiles, two half tiles along the right
edge, two along the top, and a quarter tile in the corner: $4 + 2 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 2 + 0.25 = 6.25$, and $2.5^2 = 6.25$. Tiles are a way to
*see* the number; the multiplication gives the exact value even when the tiles do not come out whole.

{/* scene: 0.2-2 — 3D widget: the same square extrudes into a cube (x³), tiles become unit cubes, orbitable; a "power" slider n = 1, 2, 3 morphs segment → square → cube */}

Beyond three the picture stops (we cannot draw a 4-dimensional cube), but the counting continues: $2^4 = 16$,
$2^5 = 32$, $2^{10} = 1024$. Every extra copy of the base multiplies once more.

## 2. The one rule

Multiply two powers of the same base. $2^3 \cdot 2^4$ is three copies of 2 times four copies of 2, which is seven
copies of 2:

$$
2^3 \cdot 2^4 = (2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2)(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2) = 2^7
$$

Check with numbers: $8 \cdot 16 = 128$ and $2^7 = 128$. In general,

$$
x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m+n}
$$

**Multiplying powers adds the exponents.** It does not multiply them: $2^3 \cdot 2^4$ is $2^7 = 128$, not
$2^{12} = 4096$. This is the most common slip with powers, so say it once more in words: *the exponent counts
copies, and putting two piles of copies together adds the counts.*

A power of a power multiplies the counts, because it repeats a repeat: $(2^3)^4$ is four copies of $2^3$, that is
$2^3 \cdot 2^3 \cdot 2^3 \cdot 2^3 = 2^{12}$.

$$
(x^m)^n = x^{m \cdot n}
$$

Check: $(2^3)^4 = 8^4 = 4096 = 2^{12}$.

Everything that follows, zero, negative and fractional exponents, is this one rule applied where counting copies
no longer makes sense on its own. We decide what $x^0$, $x^{-1}$ and $x^{1/2}$ mean *so that the rule keeps
working*. That is not a trick; it is how the definitions were chosen.

## 3. Down the ladder: zero and negative powers

Write the powers of 2 as a ladder, and look at what happens from one rung to the next below it:

$$
2^3 = 8, \qquad 2^2 = 4, \qquad 2^1 = 2
$$

Each step down **divides by the base**: $8 / 2 = 4$, $4 / 2 = 2$. Keep stepping down and do the same thing.

$$
2^0 = 2 / 2 = 1, \qquad 2^{-1} = 1 / 2 = 0.5, \qquad 2^{-2} = 0.5 / 2 = 0.25, \qquad 2^{-3} = 0.125
$$

{/* scene: 0.2-3 — powers ladder: n from 3 down to −3 on one strip of tiles, each step down divides by x (halving tiles for x = 2), making x⁰ = 1 and x⁻¹ = 1/x visible */}

In tiles: $2^2$ is a 2-by-2 square of 4 tiles; $2^1$ is a strip of 2; $2^0$ is **one tile**; $2^{-1}$ is **half a
tile**; $2^{-2}$ a quarter; $2^{-3}$ an eighth. The pattern never stops, it just moves into fractions.

So the two definitions are:

$$
x^0 = 1 \quad (x \neq 0), \qquad x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n} \quad (x \neq 0)
$$

Both are exactly what the one rule demands. For $x^0$: the rule says $x^n \cdot x^0 = x^{n+0} = x^n$, so $x^0$
must be the number that changes nothing when you multiply by it, and that number is $1$. For $x^{-1}$: the rule
says $x^1 \cdot x^{-1} = x^0 = 1$, so $x^{-1}$ must be the number that multiplies with $x$ to give $1$: it is
$1/x$. **A negative exponent means repeated division.** $x^{-1}$ *undoes* one multiplication by $x$, and
$x^{-2} = 1/x^2$ undoes two.

A worked check with the rule: $2^2 \cdot 2^{-2} = 4 \cdot 0.25 = 1 = 2^0$. Adding the exponents gives $0$; the
numbers agree.

**Misconception to drop:** "a negative exponent gives a negative number". No. $2^{-2} = 0.25$, which is positive
and small. The minus sign in the exponent says *divide*; it never flips the sign of the result. For a positive base
every power is positive.

**Misconception to drop:** "$x^0 = 0$ because there is nothing there". There is one tile there. $5^0 = 1$,
$0.3^0 = 1$, $1000^0 = 1$. The ladder lands on 1 for every base (except 0, see the warnings).

## 4. Fractional powers: the root, again

What could $x^{1/2}$ mean? Half a copy of $x$ is not something you can multiply out. Ask the rule instead:

$$
x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{1/2 + 1/2} = x^1 = x
$$

So $x^{1/2}$ is the number that, multiplied by itself, gives $x$ (the positive one, as in the previous lesson:
$-3$ also squares to $9$, but the root sign and the power $1/2$ both mean $3$). That is the square root from the
previous lesson:

$$
x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x} = \text{the side of a square of area } x
$$

Check: $9^{1/2} = 3$ because $3 \cdot 3 = 9$. $2^{1/2} \approx 1.4142$, the same number as $\sqrt 2$.

The same reasoning with three copies: $x^{1/3} \cdot x^{1/3} \cdot x^{1/3} = x^1$, so $x^{1/3}$ is the number
whose cube is $x$, the **cube root**: the edge of a cube of volume $x$. $8^{1/3} = 2$ because $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$,
and $27^{1/3} = 3$.

{/* scene: 0.2-4 — fractional power: scrub the area, the side is the square root (x^½); flip to 3D, scrub the volume, the edge is the cube root (x^⅓) */}

Any fraction follows by combining the two rules. $x^{m/n}$ is "take the $n$-th root, then the $m$-th power" (or the
other way round; for positive $x$ the result is the same):

$$
x^{m/n} = \left(x^{1/n}\right)^m
$$

Example: $8^{2/3} = (8^{1/3})^2 = 2^2 = 4$. Example with a decimal: $4^{1.5} = 4^{3/2} = (4^{1/2})^3 = 2^3 = 8$.

And the negative and fractional cases combine the same way: $d^{-1/2} = 1 / d^{1/2} = 1/\sqrt d$. For $d = 64$:
$64^{-1/2} = 1/8 = 0.125$. Keep that one in mind; it is the scaling factor inside every attention layer.

**Misconception to drop:** "$x^{1/2}$ is half of $x$". $9^{1/2}$ is $3$, not $4.5$. Halving is $x \cdot \tfrac12$; the
exponent $\tfrac12$ is a root.

## 5. Honest warnings

Three places where the ladder breaks, and what code does there.

**A negative base with a fractional exponent.** $(-8)^{1/3}$ *looks* fine: $(-2)^3 = -8$, so $-2$ is a real cube
root. But $(-4)^{1/2}$ has no real answer at all: no real number squares to $-4$, because squares forget the sign.
The general machinery of `pow` cannot tell these two apart, and treats every fractional power of a negative base as
undefined. GLSL and HLSL say `pow(x, y)` is undefined for $x < 0$ (you get NaN on most GPUs, or black pixels);
NumPy's `np.power(-8.0, 1/3)` returns `nan`; Python's `(-8.0)**(1/3)` returns a *complex* number. When you need the
real cube root, ask for it by name (`np.cbrt`) or apply the power to $|x|$ and put the sign back yourself.

**$0^0$.** The ladder for base 0 is $0^3 = 0$, $0^2 = 0$, $0^1 = 0$ and then a step down would divide by zero. So
the rule gives no answer. Most languages and NumPy return $1$ for `0**0`, by convention, because it makes polynomial
formulas like $a_0 x^0 + a_1 x^1 + \ldots$ work at $x = 0$. It is a choice, not a consequence.

**Negative exponents of 0.** $0^{-1} = 1/0$. In IEEE floating point (NumPy, shaders) that is `inf`, with a warning
in NumPy; plain Python, with ints and floats alike, raises `ZeroDivisionError` (`0.0**-1` included). The light falloff $1/r^2$ at $r = 0$ is this exact problem, which is
why every renderer clamps $r$ or adds a small constant under the fraction bar.

## 6. Where you already use this

- **Gamma.** Encoding a linear light value for the display is `pow(c, 1.0/2.2)`; decoding a display value back
  into linear light is `pow(c, 2.2)`. The round trip returns the input because
  $(c^{1/2.2})^{2.2} = c^{(1/2.2) \cdot 2.2} = c^1$. For $c = 0.5$: $0.5^{1/2.2} \approx 0.7297$, and
  $0.7297^{2.2} \approx 0.5$ again. The one rule is the reason encode and decode use reciprocal exponents. (sRGB is
  only approximately a 2.2 power curve; the idea is the same.)
- **Specular highlight.** `pow(max(0.0, dot(R, V)), n)` with $n = 8, 32, 128$. The base is a cosine between 0 and
  1. Raising a number between 0 and 1 to a higher power makes it *smaller* (the previous lesson: $0.5^2 = 0.25$), and
  $0.5^{32} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-10}$ is black. Only bases very close to 1 survive, so a bigger $n$ is a tighter
  highlight.
- **Inverse-square falloff.** The light arriving at a surface from a point light goes as $r^{-2} = 1/r^2$: twice
  the distance, a quarter of the light; four times the distance, a sixteenth. Negative exponent, repeated division.
- **Attention.** The scores (dot products of queries and keys, $QK^\top$) are divided by $\sqrt d$, where $d$ is the
  length of each query and key vector. Code writes that as `d ** -0.5`: $d^{-1/2}$, a negative *and* fractional
  exponent in one. Where the $\sqrt d$ comes from is lesson 10.2.
- **Learning-rate decay.** `lr * gamma ** step` with $\gamma = 0.5$ gives $0.1, 0.05, 0.025, \ldots$: each step is
  one more copy of $0.5$, one rung down the ladder. (A different gamma: PyTorch's name for the decay factor, nothing
  to do with the display curve above.)
- **Floating point.** A float is stored as $m \cdot 2^e$: a mantissa times a power of two, with $e$ kept as a few
  bits (8 of them in FP32, so $e$ runs from about $-126$ to $127$). In that convention $6.0 = 1.5 \cdot 2^2$;
  NumPy's `frexp` writes the same number as $0.75 \cdot 2^3$. Multiplying two floats *adds* their exponents, the one
  rule in hardware. Branch Q of this course opens the bits.

{/* scene: 0.2-5 — pow curves: y = x^n on [0, 1] for a scrubbable n (gamma 2.2 and its inverse 1/2.2, specular 8/32/128); the curve bends down below the diagonal for n > 1 and up for n < 1 */}

> **Say it simpler.** $x^n$ is $n$ copies of $x$ multiplied: a length, an area, a volume. Going one exponent down
> divides by $x$, so $x^0 = 1$ and $x^{-1} = 1/x$. Half an exponent is a root: $x^{1/2} = \sqrt x$. One rule holds it
> all together: $x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m+n}$.

## In NumPy

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

x = 2.0
print(x**1, x**2, x**3)                 # 2.0 4.0 8.0   a length, an area, a volume
print(x**0, x**-1, x**-2)               # 1.0 0.5 0.25  each step down divides by 2
print(x**0.5, np.sqrt(x))               # 1.4142135623730951 1.4142135623730951
print(8.0**(1/3), np.cbrt(8.0))         # 2.0 2.0
print(8.0**(2/3))                       # 3.9999999999999996  (8^(1/3))^2 = 4, up to rounding

print(2**3 * 2**4, 2**7)                # 128 128      x^m * x^n = x^(m+n)
print((2**3)**4, 2**12)                 # 4096 4096    (x^m)^n = x^(m*n)

n = np.arange(3, -4, -1)                # the ladder, 3 down to -3
print(n)                                # [ 3  2  1  0 -1 -2 -3]
print(2.0**n)                           # [8.    4.    2.    1.    0.5   0.25  0.125]

v = 0.5                                 # linear value: encode with 1/2.2, decode with 2.2
print(round(v**(1/2.2), 4), round((v**(1/2.2))**2.2, 4))   # 0.7297 0.5

r = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 4.0])           # inverse-square falloff
print(r**-2)                            # [1.     0.25   0.0625]

d = 64                                  # attention: 1/sqrt(d) is d^(-1/2)
print(d**-0.5, 1 / np.sqrt(d))          # 0.125 0.125

print(0.1 * 0.5**np.arange(5))          # [0.1     0.05    0.025   0.0125  0.00625]  learning-rate decay

m, e = np.frexp(6.0)                    # a float is mantissa * 2^exponent
print(m, e, m * 2.0**e)                 # 0.75 3 6.0

print((-8.0)**(1/3))                    # (1.0000000000000002+1.7320508075688772j)  not -2: see the warnings
print(np.cbrt(-8.0))                    # -2.0  the real cube root, by name
with np.errstate(invalid='ignore', divide='ignore'):
    print(np.power(-8.0, 1/3))          # nan
    print(np.float64(0.0)**-1)          # inf
print(0.0**0)                           # 1.0  by convention, not by the ladder
```

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: how pow is computed, and why x² is not pow(x, 2)</summary>

Hardware has no "raise to a power" instruction for general exponents. `pow(x, y)` is computed as
$\exp(y \cdot \ln x)$: take the logarithm of the base, multiply by the exponent, exponentiate. On a GPU that is
`exp2(y * log2(x))`, about three instructions, two of them on the slower special-function unit; a CPU math library
spends dozens of instructions on the same route to reach full double precision. The route is also why a negative base
fails in a shader: $\ln x$ does not exist for $x < 0$, so the result is undefined even when a real answer ($-2$ for
$(-8)^{1/3}$) exists. C and NumPy special-case whole exponents (`np.power(-2.0, 3.0)` is `-8.0`), so there only the
fractional exponents of a negative base fail.

Whole exponents never need that route. $x^2$ is one multiply; $x^3$ is two; a compiler turns `pow(x, 2.0)` with a
constant whole exponent into multiplies, and an integer power routine uses repeated squaring ($x^{16}$ is four
squarings, not fifteen multiplies). $x^{-2}$ is `1.0 / (x * x)`; $x^{1/2}$ is the `sqrt` instruction; $x^{-1/2}$ is
`rsqrt`, the fast inverse square root that every `normalize` uses. Write `x * x`, not `pow(x, 2.0)`: the same number,
never slower, and no logarithm of a negative input to worry about.

</details>
