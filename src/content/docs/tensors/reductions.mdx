## Collapse one axis

An elementwise operation keeps every cell. A **reduction** does the opposite: it takes a whole line of cells along one
axis and turns it into **one number**. The line is gone; one cell is left in its place. The three reductions you will
use most:

- **sum**: add the numbers up;
- **mean**: add them up and divide by how many there are (the average);
- **max**: keep the largest.

Take a sheet of 3 rows and 4 columns, think "3 samples, 4 features each":

$$
x = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 4 & 3 \\ 0 & 5 & -2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Summing along the columns of each row (axis 1, the second index $j$) gives one number per row:

$$
s_i = \sum_{j=0}^{3} x[i, j], \qquad s = (8, \; 8, \; 2)
$$

Read the $\Sigma$ as "add up $x[i, j]$ for $j = 0, 1, 2, 3$" (the sign from [Length](lesson:length)). Row 0:
$2 - 1 + 4 + 3 = 8$. The axis of size 4 has **collapsed**; the shape went from $(3, 4)$ to $(3,)$.

## The axis you name is the one that disappears

This sentence is the whole rule, and it is worth saying slowly because it feels backwards at first. `x.sum(axis=1)`
does not mean "the sums of axis 1". It means "**collapse axis 1**": walk along axis 1, add up, and remove that axis
from the shape.

- `x.sum(axis=1)`: axis 1 (size 4) disappears: $(3, 4) \to (3,)$, the row sums $(8, 8, 2)$.
- `x.sum(axis=0)`: axis 0 (size 3) disappears: $(3, 4) \to (4,)$, the column sums $(3, 7, 4, 4)$.
- `x.sum()` (NumPy writes `axis=None`): every axis disappears: $(3, 4) \to ()$, one number, $18$.

Negative axes count from the end, as negative indices do: axis $-1$ is the last axis, axis $-2$ the one before it.
For this sheet `axis=-1` is the same as `axis=1`. ML code uses `axis=-1` a lot, because "the last axis" stays the last
axis whatever the rank: the same line works for one vector, a batch of vectors or a batch of sentences.

cell at index 0 of that axis and becoming one number, then the result moving out below in its own shape. Change the

## mean: where statistics begins

The **mean** is a sum divided by the count:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} x_k
$$

For row 2 of the sheet: $(1 + 3 + 2 - 4) / 4 = 2 / 4 = 0.5$. With `axis=1` you get one mean per row,
$(2, 2, 0.5)$; with `axis=0` one mean per feature (column). This modest operation is the seed of the whole statistics
part of the course: the mean of a feature, how far each value lies from it (the variance), and why squares appear
everywhere all start here.

A CG example you know: **auto exposure**. A camera or a game engine measures the mean brightness of the frame and
scales the image so that this mean lands on a comfortable grey. For the grayscale icon in the card the mean of all 64
pixels is $139.6875$: pick the image, the mean and "all axes".

## max: the brightest pixel

`max` keeps the largest number of each line. Over the whole grayscale icon it answers "how bright is the brightest
pixel?": $231$. A surprise, maybe: it is the off-white wall, not the yellow sun. A grey value weighs the channels as the
eye does, $0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B$, and the sun $(255, 217, 51)$ has little blue, so its grey is only about $209$.

Six wall pixels share the value $231$. That is a **tie**, and it is harmless for `max`: the maximum is a *value*, and it
is $231$ whichever cell holds it. It matters only when you ask *where* the maximum is: `argmax` returns a position and,
on a tie, picks the **first** one in row-major order, here row 5, column 1. In the sheet, row 1 holds its maximum 5
twice (columns 1 and 3); the card flashes every tying cell, and the readout under it names the first.

## keepdims: keep the axis, with size 1

Sometimes you want the collapsed axis to stay, with size 1, instead of disappearing. That is `keepdims=True`: the row
sums of the sheet then have shape $(3, 1)$ instead of $(3,)$. Same three numbers, a different shape, and the shape
matters for the next operation.

The classic use is **centering**: subtract from each row its own mean. With keepdims the means form a column $(3, 1)$,
and [broadcasting](lesson:broadcasting) stretches that column across the 4 columns:

$$
x - \bar{x}_{\text{row}} : \quad (3, 4) - (3, 1) \to (3, 4)
$$

Without keepdims the means have shape $(3,)$, which is aligned on the right against the 4 columns and fails. Worse, for
a **square** matrix of shape $(3, 3)$ it does *not* fail: it silently subtracts row means from columns. keepdims makes
the intent explicit, so this bug cannot happen.

## The dot product is a reduction

Now [the dot product](lesson:dot-product) falls apart into two pieces you know. Multiply elementwise,
then sum:

$$
a \cdot b = \sum_{k} a_k \, b_k = \texttt{sum}(a * b)
$$

For $a = (2, -1, 3)$ and $b = (1, 4, 2)$: the elementwise product is $(2, -4, 6)$, and its sum is $4$. The axis shared
by $a$ and $b$ is the axis that the sum collapses: this is "the axis that disappears" from
[the tensor lesson](lesson:tensor-road). A matrix times a vector is the same with one more axis: $W$ of shape $(2, 3)$
times $v$ of shape $(3,)$ is `(W * v).sum(axis=1)`. Broadcasting stretches $v$ over both rows, the product has shape
$(2, 3)$, and summing away axis 1 leaves $(2,)$: two dot products, one per row. Libraries do not literally build the
$(2, 3)$ product (that would waste memory), but every matrix product, every einsum and every neural layer is this pair:
an elementwise product and a sum over the shared axis.

## The machine layer: the order of a sum

On paper $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$. In floating point it is not quite so: every addition is **rounded** to the
nearest representable number, and the rounding depends on how big the partial sum already is. A float32 number carries
about 7 significant digits. Between $65\,536$ and $131\,072$ the float32 numbers are spaced $0.0078125$ apart, so once
a running total is that large, each added $0.1$ has to land on that coarse grid: it actually adds
$13 \cdot 0.0078125 = 0.1015625$, about 1.6% too much, every time. At other sizes the rounding goes up or down by other
amounts, and over a million additions the errors pile up.

The experiment below adds $n$ copies of $0.1$ stored as float32, three ways, with the course's tensor core:

- **one running total, in order**: what a naive loop or a single GPU thread does;
- **pairs of pairs**: add neighbours, then neighbouring sums, and so on up a tree. Every partial sum stays the size of
  similar numbers, so each rounding is small. Parallel hardware reduces this way anyway: a GPU adds in a tree because
  thousands of threads cannot share one running total. NumPy's `sum` uses a pairwise tree too;
- **a float64 accumulator**: add in double precision and round once, at the end, to float32.

With a million numbers the running total ends at $100\,958.34$ instead of $100\,000$: almost 1% too much, and the mean
reads $0.10096$. The tree and the wide accumulator stay at $100\,000$. The same thing happens to the mean loss over a
large batch, a sum of a long vector, or the brightness of a big image, which is why careful code sums in a tree or in a
wider type, and why two correct programs can print slightly different sums: they added in different orders.

## By hand

1. For $x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ compute `x.sum(axis=0)`, `x.sum(axis=1)`, `x.mean()`
   and `x.max(axis=0)`, with their shapes.
2. A batch of images has shape $(16, 64, 64, 3)$. Which axes do you reduce for the mean colour of each image, and what
   shape do you get? And for the mean colour of the whole batch?
3. What is the shape of `x.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True)` for $x$ of shape $(2, 3, 4)$?
4. Write $a \cdot b$ for $a = (1, 2, 3)$, $b = (4, -5, 6)$ as an elementwise product followed by a sum, and compute it.
5. In float32, why can adding $0.1$ a million times one by one end far from $100\,000$, while a tree of pairwise sums
   ends almost exactly there?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $(5, 7, 9)$, shape $(3,)$; $(6, 15)$, shape $(2,)$; $21 / 6 = 3.5$, shape $()$; $(4, 5, 6)$, shape $(3,)$.
2. Axes 1 and 2 (height and width): shape $(16, 3)$, one colour per image. For the whole batch, axes 0, 1 and 2: shape
   $(3,)$.
3. $(2, 3, 1)$.
4. $a * b = (4, -10, 18)$, and its sum is $12$.
5. The running total grows large, and float32 numbers near a large value are far apart, so each added $0.1$ is rounded
   a lot, always in the same direction here. In a tree, each addition combines two partial sums of similar size, so
   every rounding is tiny compared with the numbers involved.

</details>

## In NumPy

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.array([[2, -1, 4, 3],
              [0, 5, -2, 5],
              [1, 3, 2, -4]])
print(x.sum(axis=1), x.sum(axis=0), x.sum())   # [8 8 2] [3 7 4 4] 18
print(x.mean(axis=1))                          # [2.  2.  0.5]
print(x.max(axis=1), x.argmax(axis=1))         # [4 5 3] [2 1 1]
print(x.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True).shape)     # (3, 1)

centered = x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)   # each row minus its own mean
print(centered.mean(axis=1))                   # [0. 0. 0.]

a = np.array([2, -1, 3])
b = np.array([1, 4, 2])
print((a * b).sum(), a @ b)                    # 4 4

W = np.array([[1, 0, 2],
              [0, 3, -1]])
print((W * a).sum(axis=1), W @ a)              # [ 8 -6] [ 8 -6]

v = np.full(1_000_000, 0.1, dtype=np.float32)
print(np.cumsum(v)[-1])                        # 100958.34: one running total
print(v.sum(dtype=np.float64))                 # 100000.00149011612: exact for the stored 0.1
```

</PyCell>

## What this opens

Elementwise operations and reductions are the two halves of almost every tensor computation. Put an elementwise
product and a sum over a shared axis together and you have the next lesson: batched matrix products and einsum, where
the shared index of two tensors is summed away. The mean, meanwhile, is waiting in the statistics part, where it turns
out to be the number that minimises a sum of squares.
