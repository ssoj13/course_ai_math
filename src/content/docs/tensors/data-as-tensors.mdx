## An image is H × W × C

A picture of 8 × 8 pixels with red, green and blue in every pixel is a tensor of shape $(8, 8, 3)$:

- axis 0, **H** (height): the pixel row, 0 at the top;
- axis 1, **W** (width): the pixel column, 0 on the left;
- axis 2, **C** (channel): 0 = red, 1 = green, 2 = blue.

One number is one **byte**, an integer from 0 to 255 (`uint8`), exactly what a PNG decoder hands you and what an
`RGB8` texture stores. So `x[0, 6, 1] = 217` reads: row 0, column 6, the green byte of that pixel.

In the card below the three channels stand as three planes, red in front. Drag **peel** to push them apart or press
them into one block. A bright cell is a large byte, a dark one a small byte: the sun is bright in red and green and
dark in blue, which is exactly why it looks yellow.

### Channels last or channels first

The planes are how we *think* about the channels. Memory is one strip, and there are two common orders:

- **Channels last**, $H \times W \times C$: the three bytes of a pixel are neighbours, R G B R G B … The strides are
  $(24, 3, 1)$: one row is $8 \cdot 3 = 24$ bytes. Image files, textures, OpenCV and TensorFlow use it.
- **Channels first**, $C \times H \times W$: the whole red plane, then the whole green plane, then the blue one. The
  strides are $(64, 8, 1)$: one plane is $8 \cdot 8 = 64$ bytes. PyTorch models expect it.

Tick **channels first** in the panel. The picture does not change at all: the selected byte is the same byte, with
the same value. Only its **address** changes, and the strip under the card shows why. For the green byte of
pixel (0, 6):

$$
a_{HWC} = 24 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 6 + 1 = 19
$$

$$
a_{CHW} = 64 \cdot 1 + 8 \cdot 0 + 6 = 70
$$

Same picture, same 192 numbers, two orders. Converting one into the other is a transpose of the axes plus one copy
into the new order: that is lesson [3.3](lesson:tensor-memory).

### A batch adds an axis in front

A network takes many images at once. Stack $B$ of them along a new first axis and you get $B \times H \times W \times C$.
A training batch of 32 photos of $224 \times 224$ pixels has shape $(32, 224, 224, 3)$ and holds
$32 \cdot 224 \cdot 224 \cdot 3 = 4\,816\,896$ bytes. The batch axis never mixes the images: every operation runs on
each image separately, side by side.

## Text: token ids, then rows of a table

Text is not numbers yet. A **tokenizer** cuts it into tokens and replaces each token with its **id**, an integer: the
row number of that token in a big table. The GloVe table of lesson [1.7](lesson:cosine-similarity) has 400 001 rows
(one per word, plus a padding row 0) and 300 numbers per row. Frequent words have small ids: "the" is row 1, "on" is
row 14, "cat" is row 5451.

Two sentences of 6 tokens give a tensor of ids with shape $(B, T) = (2, 6)$:

| b | tokens | ids |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | the cat sat on the mat | 1, 5451, 3224, 14, 1, 14949 |
| 1 | the man sat on the train | 1, 301, 3224, 14, 1, 1471 |

The **embedding lookup** replaces every id with its row of the table. Each id becomes a vector of $D$ numbers, so the
shape grows one axis: $(2, 6)$ becomes $(B, T, D) = (2, 6, 300)$. In NumPy this is one line, `X = E[ids]`; in PyTorch it
is the `nn.Embedding` layer.

Raise **lookups** in the card. Each step reads one id (outlined), the dashed line points at the table row it names,
and that row is copied into the next row of $X$. The card draws the first 8 of the 300 numbers (a square's size is the
size of the number, hatched = negative), so its $X$ is $(2, 6, 8)$.

## Sound: samples, then frames

A microphone measures air pressure many times per second. Each measurement is a **sample**, one float. At 8000
samples per second, 48 samples last 6 ms. The card records a pure tone, the A above middle C (440 Hz):

$$
x_n = 0.8 \sin\left(2\pi \cdot 440 \cdot \frac{n}{8000}\right)
$$

Here $n = 0, 1, \dots, 47$. For example $x_1 = 0.8 \sin(0.3456) \approx 0.271$. On its own the recording is a tensor of shape $(48,)$. Speech and
music models first cut it into short **frames** of equal length: with 12 samples per frame, the shape becomes $(4, 12)$,
four frames of 12 samples.

Change the **frame length** in the card. The bars rearrange into rows, but the strip under the card never changes:
the 48 samples stay where they are, and the new shape only says where one row ends and the next begins. That is a
**reshape**: the strides become $(12, 1)$ and sample 17 is `x[1, 5]`, because $12 \cdot 1 + 5 = 17$.

Real audio pipelines let frames overlap (a frame of 400 samples every 160 samples, for example). Overlapping frames are
still a view of the same buffer, just with a row stride smaller than the frame length; you will see why that is
possible in [the next lesson](lesson:tensor-memory).

## A mesh: two buffers

You know this one from every DCC. A mesh is two tensors:

- the **vertex buffer** $V$, shape $(N, 3)$, `float32`: one row per point, x, y, z;
- the **index buffer** $F$, shape $(F, 3)$, `int32`: one row per triangle, three vertex numbers.

The square pyramid in the card has $V$ of shape $(5, 3)$ and $F$ of shape $(6, 3)$: four side triangles and two for
the base. A triangle stores no coordinates at all; it stores three row numbers of $V$. To get its corners you look the
rows up: `V[F[f]]`, shape $(3, 3)$. For all triangles at once, `V[F]` has shape $(6, 3, 3)$.

That is the embedding lookup again. An **index tensor** (token ids, triangle corners) picks rows of a **data tensor**
(the embedding table, the vertex buffer). The GPU does the same thing when it draws an indexed mesh.

## Reading shapes

Every tensor you meet in a pipeline can be read the same way: name each axis, multiply the sizes to count the numbers,
multiply by the bytes per number to get the memory. Pick a real tensor below and read it.

<DataControls part="reader" locale={locale} client:load />

Two habits make shapes easy to read:

1. **The batch comes first**, the "smallest piece" (a channel, a feature, a coordinate) usually last.
2. **The last axis is contiguous** in the usual row-major order: its stride is 1, and each axis before it strides over
   everything after it.

## By hand

1. A batch of 16 RGB images of $32 \times 32$ pixels, channels last, `uint8`. Shape? How many bytes?
2. The same batch channels first, `float32`. Shape? How many bytes?
3. In the channels-last icon $(8, 8, 3)$, which byte lives at address 100?
4. A mesh has 1000 triangles. What are the shapes of $F$ and of `V[F]`?
5. A recording of 16 000 samples is cut into frames of 400 samples without overlap. Shape?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $(16, 32, 32, 3)$; $16 \cdot 32 \cdot 32 \cdot 3 = 49\,152$ bytes.
2. $(16, 3, 32, 32)$; the same 49 152 numbers, 4 bytes each: $196\,608$ bytes.
3. $100 = 24 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 1 + 1$: `x[4, 1, 1]`, the green byte of the pixel in row 4, column 1.
4. $F$ is $(1000, 3)$; `V[F]` is $(1000, 3, 3)$: three corners of three coordinates for every triangle.
5. $16\,000 / 400 = 40$ frames: shape $(40, 400)$.

</details>

## In NumPy

<PyCell>

```python
import numpy as np

img = np.zeros((8, 8, 3), dtype=np.uint8)           # H x W x C, channels last
img[0, 6] = (255, 217, 51)                           # the sun pixel
print(img.shape, img.strides)                        # (8, 8, 3) (24, 3, 1)

chw = np.ascontiguousarray(img.transpose(2, 0, 1))   # C x H x W, channels first
print(chw.shape, chw.strides)                        # (3, 8, 8) (64, 8, 1)
print(img[0, 6, 1], chw[1, 0, 6])                    # 217 217: same byte, other address

batch = np.stack([img, img[:, ::-1], img // 2])      # B x H x W x C
print(batch.shape, batch.nbytes)                     # (3, 8, 8, 3) 576

E = np.arange(15000 * 8, dtype=np.float32).reshape(15000, 8)  # a stand-in table: row i = 8i .. 8i+7
ids = np.array([[1, 5451, 3224, 14, 1, 14949],
                [1, 301, 3224, 14, 1, 1471]])        # B x T token ids
X = E[ids]                                           # the lookup (a gather)
print(ids.shape, X.shape)                            # (2, 6) (2, 6, 8)
print(X[0, 1, 0], np.array_equal(X[0, 0], X[1, 4]))  # 43608.0 True

t = np.arange(48) / 8000                             # 48 sample times at 8 kHz
x = (0.8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t)).astype(np.float32)
frames = x.reshape(-1, 12)                           # 4 frames of 12 samples
print(frames.shape, np.shares_memory(frames, x))     # (4, 12) True

V = np.array([[-1, 0, -1], [1, 0, -1], [1, 0, 1], [-1, 0, 1], [0, 1.5, 0]], dtype=np.float32)
F = np.array([[1, 0, 4], [2, 1, 4], [3, 2, 4], [0, 3, 4], [0, 1, 2], [0, 2, 3]], dtype=np.int32)
print(V.shape, F.shape, V[F].shape)                  # (5, 3) (6, 3) (6, 3, 3)
print(V[F[4]].tolist())                              # [[-1.0, 0.0, -1.0], [1.0, 0.0, -1.0], [1.0, 0.0, 1.0]]
```

<details>
<summary>Machine layer: why the channel order is a performance choice</summary>

Both orders hold the same bytes, so the choice is about which numbers a computation reads together. A pixel shader
reads all channels of one pixel at once: channels last keeps them in one memory access. A convolution in a network
mixes the same channel over a neighbourhood of pixels: channels first keeps each plane in one long run. GPU libraries
pick whichever their fastest kernels want, and frameworks convert between the two with a transpose plus a copy.

The index buffer is the same trade on the GPU: storing each shared corner once in $V$ and naming it from $F$ saves
memory, and lets the GPU reuse an already shaded corner (its post-transform vertex cache) instead of running the vertex
shader again for every triangle that touches it.

</details>
